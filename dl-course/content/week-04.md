[Week 3](week-03.html) taught you how to drive the training loss down. This week is about the quantity you actually care about: performance on data the model has never seen. A modern network has enough capacity to memorize its training set, including pure noise, so a low training loss by itself is nearly meaningless. What separates a model that works from one that merely fits is a combination of statistical ideas (the bias-variance trade-off, regularization, data augmentation) and unglamorous engineering practice (careful splits, logging, sanity checks, reproducible runs).

It produces a plausible-looking loss curve that is just a bit worse than it should be, and the cause might be a learning rate, a data bug, a missing `model.eval()`, or a label leak. ## Generalization: what we are actually trying to do {#generalization}

Training a model means minimizing the **empirical risk**, the average loss over a finite training set $\{(\boldsymbol{x}_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$ drawn from some unknown distribution $\mathcal{D}$:

$$
\hat{R}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \ell\big(f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}_i), y_i\big).
$$

What we care about is the **population risk** (also called expected risk or generalization error) $R(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \E_{(\boldsymbol{x},y)\sim\mathcal{D}}[\ell(f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}), y)]$. The difference $R(\boldsymbol{\theta}) - \hat{R}(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ is the **generalization gap**. We can never compute $R$, but we can estimate it with data the optimizer did not touch, which is the entire purpose of the validation and test sets discussed [later this week](#splits).

Two things can go wrong, and they have opposite remedies:

- **Underfitting**: the model cannot even fit the training data well. Training error is high. The fix is more capacity, better optimization, longer training, or less regularization.
- **Overfitting**: the model fits the training data (including its noise) at the expense of new data. Training error is low, validation error is much higher. The fix is more data, a simpler model, more regularization, or better augmentation.

The tension between these two failure modes is called the bias-variance trade-off, and it is worth deriving once, carefully.

## The bias-variance trade-off {#bias-variance}

Suppose the data are generated by $y = g(\boldsymbol{x}) + \varepsilon$, where $g$ is the true function and $\varepsilon$ is noise with $\E[\varepsilon] = 0$ and $\Var(\varepsilon) = \sigma^2$, independent of everything else. You draw a random training set $S$ of size $n$ and fit a model; call the resulting predictor $\hat f_S$. Because $S$ is random, $\hat f_S(\boldsymbol{x})$ is a random variable at every fixed test input $\boldsymbol{x}$. The bias-variance decomposition says how its expected squared error splits up.

:::math Derivation: bias-variance decomposition of squared error
Fix a test point $\boldsymbol{x}$ and write $\hat f = \hat f_S(\boldsymbol{x})$, $\bar f = \E_S[\hat f]$ (the average prediction over training sets), $g = g(\boldsymbol{x})$, and $y = g + \varepsilon$ for a fresh noisy label. The expectation is over both the training set $S$ and the test noise $\varepsilon$.

Add and subtract $g$ inside the square:
$$
\E\big[(y - \hat f)^2\big] = \E\big[(\varepsilon + g - \hat f)^2\big] = \E[\varepsilon^2] + 2\,\E[\varepsilon]\,\E[g - \hat f] + \E\big[(g - \hat f)^2\big].
$$
The cross term vanishes because $\varepsilon$ has zero mean and is independent of $\hat f$. So the error is $\sigma^2 + \E[(g - \hat f)^2]$. Now add and subtract $\bar f$:
$$
\E\big[(g - \hat f)^2\big] = \E\big[(g - \bar f + \bar f - \hat f)^2\big] = (g - \bar f)^2 + 2(g-\bar f)\,\E[\bar f - \hat f] + \E\big[(\bar f - \hat f)^2\big].
$$
Since $\E[\bar f - \hat f] = 0$ by definition of $\bar f$, the cross term disappears again and we obtain
$$
\boxed{\;\E\big[(y - \hat f)^2\big] = \underbrace{(g - \bar f)^2}_{\text{bias}^2} + \underbrace{\E\big[(\hat f - \bar f)^2\big]}_{\text{variance}} + \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{irreducible noise}}\;}
$$
:::

Read the three terms as follows.

- **Bias$^2$** measures how far the *average* learned function is from the truth. It is a property of the hypothesis class and the learning procedure: a straight line fit to a sine wave has large bias no matter how much data you give it.
- **Variance** measures how much the learned function changes from one training set to the next. A flexible model that chases noise in each sample has large variance.
- **Noise** $\sigma^2$ is the floor: no model can beat it under squared loss.

Increasing capacity typically lowers bias and raises variance, producing the familiar U-shaped test-error curve.

### Underfitting, overfitting and learning curves {#under-over-fitting}

In practice you never see bias and variance directly. You see two curves: training loss and validation loss. Their relationship is the most important diagnostic in the course.

| Training loss | Validation loss | Gap | Diagnosis | First things to try |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| high | high | small | underfitting (high bias) | bigger model, train longer, higher LR, less regularization, better features |
| low | high | large | overfitting (high variance) | more data, augmentation, weight decay, dropout, early stopping, smaller model |
| low | low | small | good fit | stop tuning, or move on to a harder problem |
| high | much higher | huge | train/validation mismatch or a bug | check preprocessing, split leakage, `model.eval()` |

A second diagnostic is the **learning curve**: error versus *training set size*. If validation error is still dropping steeply as you add data, more data will help (high variance). If both curves have flattened at the same high value, more data will not help; you need a better model (high bias).

### Capacity is not just parameter count {#capacity}

The textbook way to talk about capacity is through VC dimension or Rademacher complexity, which grow with the number of parameters. Those bounds are nearly vacuous for modern networks. In practice, **effective capacity** depends on the architecture, the optimizer, the amount of training, the data augmentation and every regularizer. Zhang et al. (2017, "Understanding deep learning requires rethinking generalization") showed that standard networks trained with SGD can fit CIFAR-10 images with *randomly assigned labels* to zero training error, yet the very same architecture generalizes well on the true labels. So the networks are certainly able to memorize, and something about the interplay of data, optimizer and architecture makes them prefer solutions that generalize when real structure exists. Regularization is the name for all of the levers that shape this preference.

## Double descent {#double-descent}

The classical U-curve predicts that test error should explode once a model has enough parameters to interpolate the training data (zero training error). Practitioners had long noticed that neural networks ignore this advice: bigger networks often generalize better. Belkin, Hsu, Ma and Mandal (2019, PNAS, "Reconciling modern machine-learning practice and the classical bias-variance trade-off") put the two together in a single picture, the **double descent curve**: as capacity grows, test error first follows the classical U, peaks near the **interpolation threshold** where the model can just barely fit the training set exactly, and then falls again in the over-parameterized regime. Nakkiran et al. (2020, ICLR, "Deep Double Descent") showed the same shape for CNNs, ResNets and transformers, and also as a function of training time and of the number of training samples.

{{figure: week04-double-descent | Double descent (schematic). Test error peaks where the number of parameters $p$ is about the number of training points $n$ and then decreases again in the over-parameterized regime.}}

### Why does the peak appear? A concrete model {#double-descent-why}

The cleanest way to see it is linear regression with $p$ random features and $n$ training points. When $p < n$ you solve ordinary least squares. When $p > n$ there are infinitely many exact fits, and gradient descent started from zero converges to the one with the **smallest norm**, which is given by the pseudo-inverse: $\hat{\boldsymbol{w}} = \mathbf{Z}^{+}\boldsymbol{y}$ for a feature matrix $\mathbf{Z}\in\R^{n\times p}$.

Near $p = n$ the feature matrix is square and nearly singular: its smallest singular values are tiny. The pseudo-inverse multiplies the label noise by $1/\sigma_{\min}$, which is enormous, so the fitted weights are huge and the test predictions oscillate wildly. That is the peak. Far beyond $p = n$ the smallest nonzero singular value grows again, the minimum-norm solution becomes gentle, and the extra directions act like implicit regularization: the model interpolates the noise but spreads its effect thinly across many directions where it barely perturbs the predictions.

Here is a real experiment (random ReLU features of 5-dimensional Gaussian inputs, $n=40$ training points, label noise $\sigma=0.5$, minimum-norm least squares, median test MSE over 30 draws):

```python:double_descent.py
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
n, d, sigma = 40, 5, 0.5
beta = rng.standard_normal(d) / np.sqrt(d)

def make_data(m):
    X = rng.standard_normal((m, d))
    y = np.tanh(2 * X @ beta) + sigma * rng.standard_normal(m)
    return X, y

for p in [10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 80, 200, 800]:          # number of random ReLU features
    test_err = []
    for _ in range(30):
        X, y = make_data(n)
        Xt, yt = make_data(500)
        W = rng.standard_normal((d, p))                 # fixed random first layer
        Z, Zt = np.maximum(X @ W, 0) / np.sqrt(p), np.maximum(Xt @ W, 0) / np.sqrt(p)
        w = np.linalg.pinv(Z) @ y                       # min-norm solution (OLS when p < n)
        test_err.append(np.mean((Zt @ w - yt) ** 2))
    print(f"p={p:4d}  median test MSE = {np.median(test_err):8.3f}")
```

### Practical consequences {#double-descent-practice}

- **Epoch-wise double descent** (Nakkiran et al.): with label noise, validation error can fall, rise, then fall again as you train longer. Early stopping at the first minimum is then not always optimal, and you should look at the whole curve before deciding.
- **Sample-wise non-monotonicity**: in the critical regime, adding data can increase test error temporarily.
- **Regularization smooths the peak.** Weight decay, label smoothing and augmentation all damp the interpolation spike. Models you train with these tools usually do not show a dramatic peak.
- **Do not over-interpret it.** Double descent describes the behavior of interpolating models. It does not mean that you may ignore regularization; the models you deploy are nearly always trained with several of the tools in this chapter.

## Weight decay: L2 and L1 regularization {#weight-decay}

The most widely used regularizer is also the simplest: add a penalty on the size of the weights to the loss. The intuition is that a function that changes slowly is simpler than one that changes quickly, and in a network with bounded inputs the slope of the function is controlled by the size of the weights. Small weights mean a smoother function, less able to chase noise.

For a loss $\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{w})$ and a penalty strength $\lambda>0$:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{L2}}(\boldsymbol{w}) = \mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{w}) + \frac{\lambda}{2}\norm{\boldsymbol{w}}_2^2, \qquad
\mathcal{L}_{\text{L1}}(\boldsymbol{w}) = \mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{w}) + \lambda\norm{\boldsymbol{w}}_1 .
$$

### Why it is called weight decay {#why-decay}

With the L2 penalty the gradient is $\grad\mathcal{L} + \lambda\boldsymbol{w}$, so one SGD step with learning rate $\eta$ is
$$
\boldsymbol{w} \leftarrow \boldsymbol{w} - \eta\big(\grad\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{w}) + \lambda\boldsymbol{w}\big) = (1 - \eta\lambda)\,\boldsymbol{w} - \eta\,\grad\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{w}).
$$
Every step first multiplies the weights by a factor slightly below one (they "decay") and then takes the usual gradient step. For plain SGD, L2 regularization and weight decay are the same thing. For adaptive optimizers they are not, as we will see [below](#l2-vs-wd).

### The Bayesian (MAP) view {#map-view}

Regularization has a clean probabilistic reading. The maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimate picks the parameters that maximize the posterior $p(\boldsymbol{w}\mid \text{data}) \propto p(\text{data}\mid\boldsymbol{w})\,p(\boldsymbol{w})$.

:::math Derivation: L2 is a Gaussian prior, L1 is a Laplace prior
Take the logarithm and negate to get a minimization problem:
$$
\hat{\boldsymbol{w}}_{\text{MAP}} = \argmin_{\boldsymbol{w}}\ \big[-\log p(\text{data}\mid\boldsymbol{w}) - \log p(\boldsymbol{w})\big].
$$
The first term is the negative log-likelihood, which for i.i.d. data is a sum over examples and is what we call the loss $n\hat{R}$ (squared error for Gaussian noise, cross-entropy for categorical outputs).

*Gaussian prior.* Let $p(\boldsymbol{w}) = \prod_j \mathcal{N}(w_j; 0, \tau^2)$. Then $-\log p(\boldsymbol{w}) = \frac{1}{2\tau^2}\norm{\boldsymbol{w}}_2^2 + \text{const}$. The MAP problem is $\min_{\boldsymbol{w}}\, n\hat{R}(\boldsymbol{w}) + \frac{1}{2\tau^2}\norm{\boldsymbol{w}}_2^2$. Dividing by $n$ gives L2 regularization with $\lambda = 1/(n\tau^2)$. A tighter prior (small $\tau$) means stronger regularization, and more data (large $n$) weakens the prior's relative influence.

*Laplace prior.* Let $p(\boldsymbol{w}) = \prod_j \frac{1}{2b}e^{-|w_j|/b}$. Then $-\log p(\boldsymbol{w}) = \frac{1}{b}\norm{\boldsymbol{w}}_1 + \text{const}$, giving the L1 penalty with $\lambda = 1/(nb)$. The Laplace density has a sharp peak at zero and heavier tails than the Gaussian, so it encodes the belief that most weights are exactly negligible while a few may be large.
:::

The MAP view is a useful way to *think*, but be careful: deep-network weights are not really drawn from $\mathcal{N}(0,\tau^2)$, and the prior is rarely what makes regularization work in practice. The reliable interpretation is the geometric one.

### Geometry: why L1 gives sparse solutions {#l1-l2-geometry}

Minimizing $\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{w}) + \lambda\Omega(\boldsymbol{w})$ is equivalent (for some budget $r$ depending on $\lambda$) to minimizing $\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{w})$ subject to $\Omega(\boldsymbol{w})\le r$. The constraint region of the L2 penalty is a ball; that of L1 is a diamond (a cross-polytope in higher dimensions) with corners on the coordinate axes. The loss has elliptical level sets around the unregularized optimum. The constrained solution is where the smallest level set that you can reach touches the constraint region. A smooth ball is typically touched at a generic point, so both weights shrink but neither is zero. The diamond is far more likely to be touched at a corner, where one coordinate is exactly zero.

{{figure: week04-l1-l2-geometry | Left: the L2 ball touches the loss ellipse at a generic point, so both weights shrink. Right: the L1 diamond touches it at a corner, which sets one weight exactly to zero.}}

An algebraic way to see the same fact comes from the one-dimensional problem $\min_w \frac12 (w - z)^2 + \lambda\,\Omega(w)$:

- L2: $w^\star = \dfrac{z}{1+\lambda}$. A proportional shrink, never exactly zero unless $z=0$.
- L1: $w^\star = \operatorname{sign}(z)\max(|z|-\lambda, 0)$, the **soft-threshold**. Any $|z|\le\lambda$ is mapped to exactly zero.

The demo shows both effects in two dimensions, including the elastic net, which mixes the two.

### L1 in deep learning {#l1-in-dl}

L1 is used much less than L2 for training dense networks. The reasons: it is not differentiable at zero (plain SGD with a subgradient oscillates around zero rather than landing on it, so exact sparsity needs proximal updates), its benefits for generalization are weaker, and sparsity can be obtained more effectively by [pruning](week-16.html) after training. It does appear when you want interpretable feature selection or sparse activations (for instance sparse autoencoders).

### Where to apply weight decay, and how much {#wd-practice}

- **Typical values:** $\lambda$ (as used in AdamW or SGD) between $10^{-5}$ and $10^{-1}$; $5\times10^{-4}$ for SGD on CIFAR-style ResNets, $0.01$ to $0.1$ for AdamW on transformers. Search it on a log scale.
- **Do not decay biases and normalization parameters.** Their scale parameters play a different role (shifting and rescaling), and decaying them hurts slightly. Most codebases build two parameter groups.
- **Interaction with normalization.** In a layer followed by BatchNorm the function is invariant to the scale of the weights, so the penalty cannot change what the layer computes. Instead, weight decay changes the *effective learning rate*: shrinking $\norm{\boldsymbol{w}}$ increases the angular step size for a fixed gradient. This is why weight decay still helps in BatchNorm networks.
- **Couple it to the schedule.** Because decay is applied every step, its total effect scales with the learning-rate schedule.

### L2 regularization versus decoupled weight decay {#l2-vs-wd}

[Week 3](week-03.html#adamw) introduced AdamW. The point to remember here: if you add $\frac{\lambda}{2}\norm{\boldsymbol{w}}^2$ to the loss and use Adam, the term $\lambda\boldsymbol{w}$ enters the gradient and then gets divided by $\sqrt{\hat v}$, the running estimate of gradient scale. Weights with large historical gradients are decayed *less*, and those with small gradients are decayed *more*, which is not what you intended. Loshchilov and Hutter (2019, ICLR, "Decoupled Weight Decay Regularization") proposed applying the decay directly to the weights, outside the adaptive scaling. In PyTorch, `torch.optim.AdamW(weight_decay=...)` does this; `torch.optim.Adam(weight_decay=...)` does the coupled L2 version.

```python:param_groups.py
import torch
import torch.nn as nn

def make_optimizer(model: nn.Module, lr=3e-4, weight_decay=0.05):
    """AdamW with weight decay on matrices only (no decay on biases and norm parameters)."""
    decay, no_decay = [], []
    for name, p in model.named_parameters():
        if not p.requires_grad:
            continue
        # 1-D tensors are biases and LayerNorm/BatchNorm gains; matrices and conv kernels have ndim >= 2
        (no_decay if p.ndim <= 1 else decay).append(p)
    groups = [
        {"params": decay, "weight_decay": weight_decay},
        {"params": no_decay, "weight_decay": 0.0},
    ]
    return torch.optim.AdamW(groups, lr=lr, betas=(0.9, 0.999))

model = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(784, 256), nn.LayerNorm(256), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(256, 10))
opt = make_optimizer(model)
print([len(g["params"]) for g in opt.param_groups])   # [2, 3]: two weight matrices, three 1-D tensors
```

## Dropout {#dropout}

Dropout (Hinton et al., 2012 preprint, "Improving neural networks by preventing co-adaptation of feature detectors"; Srivastava, Hinton, Krizhevsky, Sutskever and Salakhutdinov, 2014, JMLR, "Dropout: A Simple Way to Prevent Neural Networks from Overfitting") is a regularizer with a strikingly different flavor. During training, each hidden unit is independently set to zero with probability $p$. The network is forced to produce good predictions with a random subset of its units, so no unit can rely on the presence of specific other units. Hinton described the original intuition as preventing "co-adaptation": units that only work in combination.

### Inverted dropout {#inverted-dropout}

If a layer's output $\boldsymbol{h}\in\R^d$ is masked with $\boldsymbol{m}\in\{0,1\}^d$, $m_j\sim\text{Bernoulli}(1-p)$ i.i.d., the expected output is $(1-p)\boldsymbol{h}$. The original paper kept all units at test time and scaled the weights by $1-p$. Modern implementations use **inverted dropout**, which scales at *training* time so that inference needs no modification:

$$
\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}} = \frac{\boldsymbol{m}\odot\boldsymbol{h}}{1-p}, \qquad \E[\tilde h_j] = \frac{(1-p)\,h_j}{1-p} = h_j.
$$

At evaluation time dropout is the identity. The variance of each term is $\Var(\tilde h_j) = \frac{p}{1-p}h_j^2$, which grows quickly as $p\to1$; this is why $p$ above about $0.5$ is rarely used.

```python:dropout_numpy.py
import numpy as np

class Dropout:
    """Inverted dropout layer (NumPy), with forward and backward."""
    def __init__(self, p=0.5, seed=0):
        assert 0.0 <= p < 1.0
        self.p, self.training = p, True
        self.rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)

    def forward(self, h):
        if not self.training or self.p == 0.0:
            return h                                     # identity at eval time
        self.mask = (self.rng.random(h.shape) >= self.p) / (1.0 - self.p)  # 0 or 1/(1-p)
        return h * self.mask

    def backward(self, grad_out):
        if not self.training or self.p == 0.0:
            return grad_out
        return grad_out * self.mask                      # same mask routes the gradient

# Check: the mean over many masks matches the deterministic output
h = np.linspace(0.5, 1.5, 100)
d = Dropout(p=0.5)
samples = np.stack([d.forward(h) for _ in range(20000)])
print("max |E[drop(h)] - h| =", np.abs(samples.mean(0) - h).max())       # about 0.02
print("empirical var of unit 0:", samples[:, 0].var(), " theory:", 0.5/0.5 * h[0] ** 2)
```

### The ensemble view {#dropout-ensemble}

A network with $d$ droppable units defines $2^d$ thinned sub-networks that share weights. Training with dropout is a stochastic approximation to training all of them at once; using the full network at test time approximates averaging their predictions. For a single linear layer followed by softmax the "weight scaling" rule is exact in the geometric mean sense, and for deep nonlinear networks it is an approximation that works well in practice. A more faithful but expensive estimate is **Monte Carlo dropout**: keep dropout on at test time, average $T$ stochastic predictions, and use their spread as a rough uncertainty measure (Gal and Ghahramani, 2016, ICML, "Dropout as a Bayesian Approximation"; the Bayesian interpretation is debated, but the procedure is useful as a cheap ensemble).

Another interpretation is that dropout adds multiplicative noise to the activations, and for linear models this equals an adaptive L2 penalty on the weights, one that depends on the feature scales (Wager, Wang and Liang, 2013, NeurIPS, "Dropout Training as Adaptive Regularization").

### Where dropout helps and where it does not {#dropout-practice}

- **Fully connected layers** of classification heads and MLPs: classic use, $p=0.5$ in the early literature.
- **Convolutional layers:** neighboring activations are strongly correlated, so dropping single units does little; the information survives through neighbors. Spatial variants (drop whole channels) or DropBlock work better.
- **Transformers:** small rates ($0.1$) on attention probabilities, residual branches and embeddings. Many large pretrained models use 0, because with a single pass over a huge dataset there is nothing to overfit.
- **With BatchNorm:** dropout before BatchNorm changes the variance statistics between train and eval ("variance shift", Li et al. 2019), so people tend to use one or the other, or place dropout after the last norm layer.
- **RNNs:** naive dropout on the recurrent connection destroys memory; use the same mask across time steps (variational dropout, Gal and Ghahramani 2016).
- **Needs more epochs.** Dropout makes each step noisier, so training converges more slowly; budget for it.

### DropConnect {#dropconnect}

DropConnect (Wan, Zeiler, Zhang, LeCun and Fergus, 2013, ICML, "Regularization of Neural Networks using DropConnect") masks individual **weights** instead of activations: $\boldsymbol{y} = \sigma\big((\mathbf{M}\odot\mathbf{W})\boldsymbol{x}\big)$ with $M_{ij}\sim\text{Bernoulli}(1-p)$. Dropout is the special case where an entire column of $\mathbf{M}$ is shared (all weights leaving a dropped input unit are removed together). DropConnect has more randomness per layer, since $d_{\text{in}}d_{\text{out}}$ mask entries are sampled instead of $d_{\text{in}}$, and it is costlier because the mask differs per example (it cannot be implemented as one matrix multiply on the batch unless you share the mask across the batch). The gains reported in the original paper were modest, and today it is rarely used outside of specialized settings.

```python:dropconnect.py
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F

class DropConnectLinear(nn.Linear):
    """Linear layer whose weights are dropped (one mask per mini-batch, a cheap variant)."""
    def __init__(self, in_features, out_features, p=0.2, bias=True):
        super().__init__(in_features, out_features, bias)
        self.p = p

    def forward(self, x):
        if not self.training or self.p == 0:
            return F.linear(x, self.weight, self.bias)
        keep = 1.0 - self.p
        mask = torch.bernoulli(torch.full_like(self.weight, keep))
        return F.linear(x, self.weight * mask / keep, self.bias)   # inverted scaling on weights

layer = DropConnectLinear(32, 16, p=0.3)
x = torch.randn(8, 32)
layer.train(); y1, y2 = layer(x), layer(x)
layer.eval();  y3, y4 = layer(x), layer(x)
print(torch.allclose(y1, y2), torch.allclose(y3, y4))          # False True
```

## Label smoothing {#label-smoothing}

With hard one-hot targets, cross-entropy is minimized only when the correct logit is infinitely larger than the others, so a model that tries to fit the labels perfectly keeps growing its logits. The result is overconfident predictions. **Label smoothing** (Szegedy et al., 2016, CVPR, "Rethinking the Inception Architecture for Computer Vision") replaces the one-hot target with a mixture of the one-hot vector and the uniform distribution over $K$ classes:

$$
\tilde{y}_k = (1-\varepsilon)\,\mathbb{1}[k=y] + \frac{\varepsilon}{K}.
$$

The loss is $-\sum_k \tilde y_k \log p_k$. With $\varepsilon=0.1$ and $K=10$ the target for the correct class is $0.91$ and every other class receives $0.01$.

:::math What the optimal logits look like
For a target distribution $\tilde{\boldsymbol y}$, cross-entropy is minimized when $\boldsymbol p=\tilde{\boldsymbol y}$, which means $z_y - z_k = \log\frac{1-\varepsilon+\varepsilon/K}{\varepsilon/K}$ for every $k\ne y$ -- a finite gap. For $\varepsilon = 0.1$, $K=10$: $\log(0.91/0.01) = \log 91 \approx 4.51$. With a hard label the optimal gap is infinite, so the gradient never vanishes and the logits keep growing.
:::

Consequences: better calibrated probabilities, tighter clusters of penultimate-layer activations (Müller, Kornblith and Hinton, 2019, NeurIPS, "When Does Label Smoothing Help?"), and a small accuracy gain on many classification tasks. Downsides: it can hurt **knowledge distillation**, because it erases the information in the "wrong" classes' relative probabilities, and it changes what a predicted probability means.

```python:label_smoothing.py
import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

def smoothed_ce(logits, target, eps=0.1):
    """Cross-entropy with label smoothing; logits (B, K), target (B,) of class indices."""
    logp = F.log_softmax(logits, dim=-1)
    nll = -logp.gather(1, target[:, None]).squeeze(1)        # loss on the true class
    uniform = -logp.mean(dim=-1)                              # cross-entropy to the uniform distribution
    return ((1 - eps) * nll + eps * uniform).mean()

logits, target = torch.randn(5, 10), torch.randint(0, 10, (5,))
print(smoothed_ce(logits, target, 0.1).item())
print(F.cross_entropy(logits, target, label_smoothing=0.1).item())   # built in since PyTorch 1.10; should match
```

## Early stopping {#early-stopping}

Early stopping is the cheapest regularizer there is: monitor the validation loss during training and keep the weights from the epoch where it was lowest. The training procedure itself does the model selection, and the number of training steps becomes a hyperparameter chosen by validation data.

For a linear model trained by gradient descent from zero initialization on a quadratic loss, early stopping is mathematically close to L2 regularization (Goodfellow, Bengio and Courville, *Deep Learning*, Ch. 7.8): directions with large curvature are learned first, directions with small curvature are learned slowly, so stopping at step $t$ leaves the small-curvature directions near zero, much like a ridge penalty with $\lambda\approx 1/(\eta t)$ does. The equivalence is exact only for that simple setting, but it conveys why stopping helps: the model has not yet had time to fit the noise.

A robust implementation has four ingredients:

1. **Patience.** Stop only after the validation metric has failed to improve for $k$ consecutive evaluations. Validation curves are noisy, so a patience of 1 stops far too early.
2. **A minimum improvement** `min_delta` so that tiny fluctuations do not reset the counter.
3. **Restore the best weights** at the end (or simply load the best checkpoint), not the last ones.
4. **Choose the monitored quantity deliberately.** Validation loss and validation accuracy can disagree: loss can start increasing from overconfidence while accuracy still improves. Use the metric you will report, and keep the loss for diagnosis.

```python:early_stopping.py
import copy

class EarlyStopping:
    """Stop when `mode`-metric has not improved by `min_delta` for `patience` evaluations."""
    def __init__(self, patience=5, min_delta=0.0, mode="min"):
        self.patience, self.min_delta, self.mode = patience, min_delta, mode
        self.best, self.bad_epochs, self.best_state = None, 0, None

    def _improved(self, v):
        if self.best is None:
            return True
        return v < self.best - self.min_delta if self.mode == "min" else v > self.best + self.min_delta

    def step(self, value, model):
        """Call once per evaluation; returns True if training should stop."""
        if self._improved(value):
            self.best, self.bad_epochs = value, 0
            self.best_state = copy.deepcopy(model.state_dict())      # remember best weights
        else:
            self.bad_epochs += 1
        return self.bad_epochs >= self.patience

    def restore(self, model):
        if self.best_state is not None:
            model.load_state_dict(self.best_state)

# Usage inside a training loop (sketch):
#   stopper = EarlyStopping(patience=5)
#   for epoch in range(max_epochs):
#       train_one_epoch(...)
#       val_loss = evaluate(...)
#       if stopper.step(val_loss, model): break
#   stopper.restore(model)
```

:::warning Early stopping uses the validation set
Because the stopping epoch is chosen with validation data, the validation score at that epoch is a slightly optimistic estimate of generalization. This is one more reason to keep a separate [test set](#splits) that you touch once.
:::

## Data augmentation {#data-augmentation}

If more data is the best regularizer, then manufacturing plausible new data is the next best. **Data augmentation** applies random label-preserving transformations to training examples so that the model sees a different variation every epoch. Each transformation encodes a piece of prior knowledge: "a cat shifted by three pixels is still a cat". The model is taught an invariance that it would otherwise have to learn from examples.

| Domain | Typical augmentations | Danger |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Images | random crop with padding, horizontal flip, color jitter, rotation, scale, RandAugment, random erasing | flipping digits or text; crops that cut out the object |
| Audio | time/pitch shift, additive noise, SpecAugment (masking time and frequency bands) | pitch shift that changes the speaker or word |
| Text | synonym replacement, back-translation, token dropout/masking | changes that flip sentiment or meaning |
| Tabular | noise injection, SMOTE for imbalance | breaking correlations between features |

Rules of thumb:

- Augment **only the training set**. Validation and test data get the deterministic preprocessing (resize, center crop, normalize) only.
- Augmentations must be **label-preserving for your task**. Rotating a "6" by 180 degrees gives a "9".
- Strong augmentation behaves like strong regularization: training loss stays higher and you must train longer. Do not read a high training loss under heavy augmentation as underfitting.
- Apply augmentation on the fly in the data loader (each epoch sees fresh variants) rather than pre-generating a fixed larger dataset.
- For a model that must be robust to a specific nuisance (lighting, noise, JPEG artifacts), include that nuisance in training.

```python:augment.py
import torch
from torch.utils.data import DataLoader
from torchvision import datasets, transforms

mean, std = (0.4914, 0.4822, 0.4465), (0.2470, 0.2435, 0.2616)       # CIFAR-10 channel statistics

train_tf = transforms.Compose([
    transforms.RandomCrop(32, padding=4),          # shift by up to 4 pixels
    transforms.RandomHorizontalFlip(),             # fine for CIFAR-10 objects, wrong for digits
    transforms.ToTensor(),
    transforms.Normalize(mean, std),
    transforms.RandomErasing(p=0.25),              # operates on tensors, so it comes after ToTensor
])
eval_tf = transforms.Compose([transforms.ToTensor(), transforms.Normalize(mean, std)])  # no randomness

train_ds = datasets.CIFAR10("data", train=True, download=True, transform=train_tf)
val_ds = datasets.CIFAR10("data", train=True, download=True, transform=eval_tf)   # same files, no augmentation

g = torch.Generator().manual_seed(0)
perm = torch.randperm(len(train_ds), generator=g)
train_idx, val_idx = perm[:45000], perm[45000:]                         # fixed split, reused everywhere
train_loader = DataLoader(torch.utils.data.Subset(train_ds, train_idx), batch_size=128, shuffle=True, num_workers=2)
val_loader = DataLoader(torch.utils.data.Subset(val_ds, val_idx), batch_size=256, num_workers=2)

xb, yb = next(iter(train_loader))
print(xb.shape, yb.shape)                          # torch.Size([128, 3, 32, 32]) torch.Size([128])
```

Notice the two dataset objects over the same files: the validation subset must be drawn from the copy with the *evaluation* transform. A frequent bug is to split a single augmented dataset, which means you validate on augmented images.

### mixup {#mixup}

**mixup** (Zhang, Cisse, Dauphin and Lopez-Paz, 2018, ICLR, "mixup: Beyond Empirical Risk Minimization") creates a training example by taking a convex combination of two examples *and* of their labels:

$$
\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}} = \lambda\boldsymbol{x}_i + (1-\lambda)\boldsymbol{x}_j, \qquad
\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}} = \lambda\boldsymbol{y}_i + (1-\lambda)\boldsymbol{y}_j, \qquad \lambda\sim\text{Beta}(\alpha,\alpha),
$$

where $\boldsymbol y$ are one-hot vectors. The prior it encodes is that **the model should behave linearly between training examples**: halfway between a cat and a dog the prediction should be halfway between "cat" and "dog". This discourages sharp, overconfident transitions and abrupt behavior off the data manifold. The hyperparameter $\alpha$ controls strength: $\text{Beta}(\alpha,\alpha)$ with $\alpha<1$ puts mass near 0 and 1 (mild mixing), $\alpha=1$ is uniform, and $\alpha>1$ concentrates around $\tfrac12$ (aggressive). Typical values: $\alpha\in[0.1, 0.4]$ for CIFAR-style data; the original paper used $\alpha=1$ for some settings.

Because the loss is linear in the target, you never need to build mixed one-hot vectors; instead compute two cross-entropies and mix them:
$$
\ell = \lambda\,\text{CE}(f(\tilde{\boldsymbol x}), y_i) + (1-\lambda)\,\text{CE}(f(\tilde{\boldsymbol x}), y_j).
$$

### CutMix {#cutmix}

Mixed images from mixup look unnatural: ghosted, low-contrast overlays. **CutMix** (Yun, Han, Oh, Chun, Choe and Yoo, 2019, ICCV, "CutMix: Regularization Strategy to Train Strong Classifiers with Localizable Features") instead cuts a rectangular patch out of image $j$ and pastes it onto image $i$, with the labels mixed in proportion to the *area*. The box has side lengths $W\sqrt{1-\lambda}$ and $H\sqrt{1-\lambda}$ and a uniformly random center, clipped to the image, and after clipping $\lambda$ is recomputed from the actual pasted area so that the label weight is exact. Every pixel remains a real, sharp pixel, and the model must recognize an object from partial views, which also helps localization.

{{figure: week04-mixup-cutmix | mixup blends all pixels; CutMix pastes a rectangular patch. In both cases the label is mixed with the same weights that apply to the image.}}

{{demo: week04-mixup | mixup and CutMix viewer | 440}}

```python:mixup_cutmix.py
import numpy as np
import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

def mixup_batch(x, y, alpha=0.2):
    """x: (B, C, H, W), y: (B,) class indices. Returns mixed x, the two label sets, and lambda."""
    lam = np.random.beta(alpha, alpha) if alpha > 0 else 1.0
    idx = torch.randperm(x.size(0), device=x.device)           # partner for every sample
    return lam * x + (1 - lam) * x[idx], y, y[idx], lam

def cutmix_batch(x, y, alpha=1.0):
    lam = np.random.beta(alpha, alpha)
    B, C, H, W = x.shape
    idx = torch.randperm(B, device=x.device)
    cut_h, cut_w = int(H * np.sqrt(1 - lam)), int(W * np.sqrt(1 - lam))
    cy, cx = np.random.randint(H), np.random.randint(W)        # box centre, may fall near the border
    y1, y2 = np.clip(cy - cut_h // 2, 0, H), np.clip(cy + cut_h // 2, 0, H)
    x1, x2 = np.clip(cx - cut_w // 2, 0, W), np.clip(cx + cut_w // 2, 0, W)
    x = x.clone()
    x[:, :, y1:y2, x1:x2] = x[idx, :, y1:y2, x1:x2]            # paste the patch from the partner image
    lam = 1 - (y2 - y1) * (x2 - x1) / (H * W)                  # exact area after clipping
    return x, y, y[idx], lam

def mixed_loss(logits, ya, yb, lam):
    return lam * F.cross_entropy(logits, ya) + (1 - lam) * F.cross_entropy(logits, yb)

x, y = torch.randn(16, 3, 32, 32), torch.randint(0, 10, (16,))
xm, ya, yb, lam = cutmix_batch(x, y)
logits = torch.randn(16, 10)
print(xm.shape, round(float(lam), 3), mixed_loss(logits, ya, yb, lam).item())
```

:::note Practical notes
Mixed-sample methods usually need longer training (they are strong regularizers), and their training accuracy is not meaningful as a metric: measure on clean validation data. They help most when the dataset is small relative to the model; they can hurt on tasks where mixed inputs have no sensible label (fine-grained retrieval, many segmentation setups) or when the training budget is very short. In the same family: Cutout/random erasing (DeVries and Taylor, 2017; Zhong et al., 2020), RandAugment (Cubuk et al., 2020) and AutoAugment (Cubuk et al., 2019).
:::

### How the regularizers interact {#regularizer-interactions}

Regularizers are not additive in a simple way. They are all trading bias for variance, so stacking many of them on a small model eventually produces underfitting. A sensible order of operations for a new problem: (1) get a model that can overfit; (2) add data augmentation, which is almost always free accuracy; (3) tune weight decay; (4) add dropout, label smoothing or mixed-sample methods if a gap remains; (5) tune the training length with the learning-rate schedule rather than relying only on early stopping. The [lab](#lab) includes an ablation that quantifies each piece.

## Hyperparameter search {#hyperparameter-search}

Everything in this chapter has knobs: learning rate, weight decay, dropout rate, label-smoothing $\varepsilon$, batch size, width, depth, schedule length. **Hyperparameters** are the quantities you do not learn by gradient descent. The search problem: pick the configuration with the best *validation* score within a compute budget.

### Which hyperparameters matter most {#which-hparams}

Sensitivity is wildly uneven. For most vision and language models the order of importance is roughly: learning rate (by far the most important), then batch size and schedule length, then weight decay, then everything else (momentum $\beta$, Adam $\epsilon$, dropout, architecture width). Karpathy's advice is to fix a reasonable default for most knobs and spend the budget on the learning rate. Learning rate, weight decay and decay-like quantities should be searched on a **logarithmic scale**: the difference between $10^{-3}$ and $2\times10^{-3}$ matters, the difference between $0.1$ and $0.101$ does not.

### Grid search versus random search {#grid-random}

**Grid search** tries every combination on a regular lattice. It scales exponentially with the number of hyperparameters and, more subtly, wastes budget when only a few hyperparameters matter: a $3\times3$ grid tests only 3 distinct values of each hyperparameter. **Random search** samples every hyperparameter independently from its range; 9 random trials test 9 distinct values of each. Bergstra and Bengio (2012, JMLR, "Random Search for Hyper-Parameter Optimization") showed this is substantially more efficient when the response depends strongly on only a few of the dimensions, which is typical.

### Practical protocol: coarse to fine, with successive halving {#hparam-protocol}

1. Start with a **coarse random search** over wide log-uniform ranges, using short runs (a few epochs or a fraction of the final budget).
2. Look at the results (a plot of score versus each hyperparameter is worth a hundred tables); **narrow the ranges** around the good region; repeat with longer runs.
3. Re-train the best 1 to 3 configurations to the full budget with several seeds to separate real differences from noise.

Allocate compute adaptively: **successive halving** (Jamieson and Talwalkar, 2016) trains many configurations for a short time, keeps the best fraction, gives them more budget, and repeats. **Hyperband** (Li, Jamieson, DeSalvo, Rostamizadeh and Talwalkar, 2018, JMLR) runs successive halving with several different trade-offs between the number of configurations and the budget each gets.

```python:random_search.py
import numpy as np

def sample_config(rng):
    return {
        "lr": 10 ** rng.uniform(-4, -1),              # log-uniform in [1e-4, 1e-1]
        "weight_decay": 10 ** rng.uniform(-6, -2),
        "dropout": rng.choice([0.0, 0.1, 0.3, 0.5]),
        "width": int(rng.choice([64, 128, 256, 512])),
    }

def successive_halving(train_eval, n_configs=27, min_epochs=2, eta=3, seed=0):
    """train_eval(config, epochs) -> validation score (higher is better)."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    configs = [sample_config(rng) for _ in range(n_configs)]
    epochs = min_epochs
    while len(configs) > 1:
        scores = [train_eval(c, epochs) for c in configs]
        keep = max(1, len(configs) // eta)
        order = np.argsort(scores)[::-1][:keep]                  # best first
        print(f"epochs={epochs:3d}: {len(configs):2d} configs, best score {max(scores):.3f}")
        configs = [configs[i] for i in order]
        epochs *= eta                                           # survivors get a larger budget
    return configs[0]

# Toy stand-in for training so the code runs: score peaks near lr = 3e-3, improves with epochs
def fake_train_eval(cfg, epochs):
    return -abs(np.log10(cfg["lr"]) + 2.5) - 0.2 * abs(np.log10(cfg["weight_decay"]) + 4) + 0.1 * np.log(epochs)

best = successive_halving(fake_train_eval)
print(best)
```

### Bayesian optimization {#bayesian-optimization}

When each training run is expensive, it pays to *learn* from earlier trials. **Bayesian optimization** fits a cheap probabilistic surrogate model to the observed (configuration, score) pairs, then uses an **acquisition function** to decide where to run next, balancing exploitation (try near the best so far) against exploration (try where the surrogate is uncertain).

The classic surrogate is a Gaussian process (GP) giving a posterior mean $\mu(\boldsymbol x)$ and standard deviation $s(\boldsymbol x)$ at any configuration $\boldsymbol{x}$. A popular acquisition function is **expected improvement** over the best score so far $f^\star$ (for maximization):

$$
\text{EI}(\boldsymbol{x}) = \E\big[\max(f(\boldsymbol{x}) - f^\star, 0)\big] = (\mu - f^\star)\,\Phi(z) + s\,\varphi(z), \qquad z = \frac{\mu - f^\star}{s},
$$

where $\Phi,\varphi$ are the standard normal CDF and density. The first term rewards a high predicted mean, the second rewards uncertainty. Tools implementing this idea: Optuna (default sampler TPE, a tree-structured Parzen estimator rather than a GP), Ray Tune, Weights & Biases Sweeps, and Ax/BoTorch. Snoek, Larochelle and Adams (2012, NeurIPS, "Practical Bayesian Optimization of Machine Learning Algorithms") is the paper that popularized GP-based tuning for neural networks.

Bayesian optimization shines with few (roughly under 20) continuous hyperparameters and expensive evaluations; it is less helpful for high-dimensional or heavily conditional spaces, and in a *highly parallel* regime random search plus successive halving is hard to beat. Whatever you use, remember that the more configurations you evaluate on the same validation set, the more the best validation score is [optimistically biased](#splits).

### The learning-rate range test {#lr-range-test}

Because the learning rate matters most, there is a dedicated, very cheap procedure for finding a good range: the **LR range test** (Smith, 2017, WACV, "Cyclical Learning Rates for Training Neural Networks", and popularized by the fast.ai library as `lr_find`). Train for a few hundred steps while increasing the learning rate exponentially from tiny ($10^{-7}$) to large ($10$), record a smoothed loss at each step, and plot loss against learning rate on a log axis. Three regions appear: a flat region where the rate is too small to make progress, a steep descent, and a blow-up where the loss shoots up. A good maximum learning rate is roughly one order of magnitude below the point where the loss minimum occurs (commonly picked at the steepest descent, or about a tenth of the divergence point).

```python:lr_range_test.py
import copy
import torch
import torch.nn as nn

def lr_range_test(model, loader, loss_fn, lr_min=1e-7, lr_max=10.0, steps=200, beta=0.98, device="cpu"):
    """Returns (lrs, smoothed_losses). Works on a copy; the original model is untouched."""
    model = copy.deepcopy(model).to(device).train()
    opt = torch.optim.SGD(model.parameters(), lr=lr_min, momentum=0.9)
    gamma = (lr_max / lr_min) ** (1.0 / (steps - 1))             # multiplicative LR step
    lrs, losses, avg, best = [], [], 0.0, float("inf")
    data = iter(loader)
    for i in range(steps):
        try:
            x, y = next(data)
        except StopIteration:
            data = iter(loader); x, y = next(data)
        x, y = x.to(device), y.to(device)
        lr = lr_min * gamma ** i
        for g in opt.param_groups:
            g["lr"] = lr
        loss = loss_fn(model(x), y)
        avg = beta * avg + (1 - beta) * loss.item()
        smoothed = avg / (1 - beta ** (i + 1))                   # bias-corrected EMA, as in Adam
        if i > 10 and (smoothed > 4 * best or not torch.isfinite(loss)):
            break                                                # diverged: stop early
        best = min(best, smoothed)
        lrs.append(lr); losses.append(smoothed)
        opt.zero_grad(set_to_none=True)
        loss.backward()
        opt.step()
    return lrs, losses

if __name__ == "__main__":
    net = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(20, 64), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(64, 3))
    X, Y = torch.randn(512, 20), torch.randint(0, 3, (512,))
    loader = torch.utils.data.DataLoader(torch.utils.data.TensorDataset(X, Y), batch_size=64, shuffle=True)
    lrs, losses = lr_range_test(net, loader, nn.CrossEntropyLoss(), 1e-5, 10, 100)
    print(f"{len(lrs)} steps, picked lr ~ {lrs[min(range(len(losses)), key=losses.__getitem__)] / 10:.2e}")
```

## Debugging neural networks {#debugging}

Software bugs usually crash. Neural-network bugs usually *train*, just worse than they should. The model learns something, the loss goes down a bit, and you cannot tell whether the result reflects the method or a mistake. Karpathy's "A Recipe for Training Neural Networks" (2019) is the standard reference for the mindset: build up complexity slowly, verify every step, and never trust a result you cannot explain. The checks below are ordered from cheapest to most expensive; run them in this order every time you start a new model.

### Step 0: look at the data {#look-at-data}

Spend ten minutes looking at raw examples before writing a model. Print shapes, dtypes, value ranges, and class counts. Visualize images *after* the transformation pipeline (the tensor that actually enters the model), with labels. Look for duplicates, mislabeled examples, class imbalance, outliers and, for sequences, padding conventions. Many "model" problems are data problems.

### Step 1: check the initial loss {#initial-loss}

At initialization with small weights, the network's outputs are nearly uniform, so for $K$-class cross-entropy the loss should be close to $\ln K$: $2.303$ for 10 classes, $6.908$ for 1000 classes, $\ln(50000)\approx 10.8$ for a 50k-token vocabulary. For mean-squared-error regression on standardized targets expect about $1$. If you see something far away (say 25 for CIFAR-10), the last layer's scale is wrong, the loss is applied to probabilities instead of logits, or labels and outputs are misaligned. Also check that **the loss decreases when you add weight decay or regularization to zero** and that the regularized loss at init equals the data loss plus the expected penalty.

### Step 2: overfit a single batch {#overfit-single-batch}

Take one small batch (say 8 to 32 examples), turn off augmentation, dropout and weight decay, and train on only that batch for a few hundred steps. A correct model with enough capacity must drive the loss to nearly zero and reach 100% accuracy on it. If it cannot, something fundamental is wrong. This single test catches most bugs: a wrong loss, a detached tensor, frozen parameters, labels not matching inputs, a learning rate wildly off, a missing `zero_grad`, a broken optimizer step, or a shape-broadcast mistake.

```python:overfit_one_batch.py
import torch
import torch.nn as nn

torch.manual_seed(0)
model = nn.Sequential(nn.Flatten(), nn.Linear(784, 128), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(128, 10))
x = torch.randn(16, 1, 28, 28)                          # stand-in for one real batch of MNIST
y = torch.randint(0, 10, (16,))
opt = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=1e-3)
loss_fn = nn.CrossEntropyLoss()

print("initial loss:", loss_fn(model(x), y).item(), " (expect about ln 10 = 2.303)")
for step in range(300):
    opt.zero_grad(set_to_none=True)
    loss = loss_fn(model(x), y)
    loss.backward()
    opt.step()
    if step % 50 == 0 or step == 299:
        acc = (model(x).argmax(1) == y).float().mean().item()
        print(f"step {step:3d}  loss {loss.item():.4f}  acc {acc:.2f}")
assert loss.item() < 0.05, "cannot memorize 16 examples: there is a bug"
```

Even random labels should be memorizable here, which is exactly why the test is strong: it checks the mechanics (forward, loss, backward, update) without requiring any real structure to be learned.

### Step 3: gradient and invariance checks {#gradient-checks}

If you wrote a custom layer or loss, verify its backward pass with **finite differences** (from [Week 2](week-02.html)): compare the analytic gradient with $\frac{f(\theta+h)-f(\theta-h)}{2h}$ in double precision. More generally, three cheap invariance tests catch layer-wiring bugs:

- **Batch independence.** Per-example outputs must not depend on the other examples in the batch (in eval mode). Compute the gradient of the sum of the output of example 3 with respect to the *input* and check that it is zero for every other example. Mixing across the batch (a wrong `view` or `transpose`) fails this.
- **Permutation or shift equivariance** where your architecture promises it.
- **Eval determinism.** In eval mode two forward passes give identical outputs.

```python:batch_independence.py
import torch
import torch.nn as nn

def check_batch_independence(model, x, i=3):
    """Gradient of example i's output w.r.t. the other examples must be exactly 0."""
    model.eval()
    x = x.clone().requires_grad_(True)
    out = model(x)
    out[i].sum().backward()
    others = torch.cat([x.grad[:i], x.grad[i + 1:]])
    return others.abs().max().item()

net = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(10, 32), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(32, 4))
print("leak (should be 0):", check_batch_independence(net, torch.randn(8, 10)))

class Leaky(nn.Module):                                   # a deliberately buggy layer that normalizes over the batch
    def forward(self, x):
        return x - x.mean(dim=0, keepdim=True)
print("leak in buggy model:", check_batch_independence(nn.Sequential(Leaky(), nn.Linear(10, 4)), torch.randn(8, 10)))
```

### Step 4: instrument the training run {#instrumentation}

Log more than the loss. The quantities that expose problems early:

- **Gradient norm** (global, and per layer). Exploding or vanishing norms are visible long before the loss reacts.
- **Update-to-weight ratio** $\norm{\Delta\boldsymbol w}/\norm{\boldsymbol w}$ per layer, which should be around $10^{-3}$ for healthy training with a reasonable learning rate (a rule of thumb, from Karpathy's course notes).
- **Activation statistics** (mean, std, fraction of zeros after ReLU). A layer with 100% dead ReLUs is a failed layer.
- **Learning rate** (it is surprising how often the scheduler is not doing what you think).
- **Throughput** and data-loading time, so you notice when the GPU is starved.
- **Sample predictions** on a fixed validation batch, as images or text, not just numbers.

### Common bugs, with symptoms {#common-bugs}

| Bug | Typical symptom | How to find it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Forgot `optimizer.zero_grad()` | loss unstable or diverges, gradients grow over steps | print the grad norm each step |
| Forgot `model.eval()` / `torch.no_grad()` for validation | validation worse than expected, differs between runs; BatchNorm and dropout still active | assert `not model.training` in `evaluate` |
| Softmax applied before `CrossEntropyLoss` | loss stalls at a high value, training slow | read the docs: it expects logits |
| Wrong label/input alignment after shuffling | stuck at chance, or train accuracy good but val at chance | overfit one batch; plot a batch with its labels |
| Normalization mismatch between train and test | big train/test gap that augmentation does not explain | save and reuse the exact same mean/std |
| Data leakage (duplicates or temporal leaks across splits) | validation suspiciously great, collapse in production | hash examples; split by group or time |
| Augmenting the validation set | noisy, pessimistic validation | separate transform pipelines |
| Wrong shape broadcasting (`(B,)` vs `(B,1)` in MSE) | loss does not go down, or a PyTorch warning | check shapes of every loss argument |
| Learning rate too high or too low | NaN or flat; see [loss curves](#loss-curve-diagnosis) | LR range test |
| Frozen or missing parameters in the optimizer | one part of the model never changes | compare parameters before and after one step |
| Dead ReLUs / bad init | loss stuck near $\ln K$ | activation statistics, [He init](week-03.html#initialization) |
| `inf`/`NaN` from `log(0)` or overflow | sudden NaN | `torch.autograd.set_detect_anomaly(True)`, use `logits`-based losses |
| In-place ops breaking autograd, or `.detach()` in the wrong place | error, or silently no gradient | check that `p.grad is not None` for all parameters |

### Loss-curve diagnosis {#loss-curve-diagnosis}

With the sanity checks passed, read the curves (see the figure [above](#under-over-fitting)):

- **Flat at about $\ln K$ from step 0:** nothing is learning. Suspect learning rate (too small, or the scheduler's warmup never ends), initialization, a detached graph or labels that carry no information.
- **Fast initial drop then plateau high:** underfitting. Increase capacity, train longer, reduce regularization, check the learning rate (a too-high rate bounces at a plateau).
- **Training falls, validation turns up:** overfitting. More augmentation, weight decay, dropout, smaller model, early stopping, more data.
- **Noisy, spiking or diverging:** learning rate too high, missing gradient clipping, bad batch, numerical instability. Lower LR, add warmup and clipping.
- **Validation below training loss:** usually benign when regularization (dropout, augmentation) is active only during training, or when training loss is averaged over the epoch while validation is computed at the end. It becomes suspicious when it persists with no regularization: check for leakage.
- **Sudden jumps after a flat period:** often a learning-rate schedule step, or a "grokking"-like generalization after long training on small algorithmic data.
- **Training and validation both good but test bad:** distribution shift between splits, or you tuned on the validation set so much that it leaked.

:::tip The debugging order
(1) Look at data. (2) Initial loss $\approx\ln K$. (3) Overfit one batch. (4) Overfit a small subset (say 1,000 examples) to 100% training accuracy. (5) Scale to the full data and train a simple baseline. (6) Only then add capacity and regularization, **one change at a time**. When something breaks, you know which change did it.
:::

## Experiment management {#experiment-management}

A result you cannot reproduce is an anecdote. Deep-learning experiments are expensive and noisy, so a little discipline pays for itself many times over.

### Train, validation and test discipline {#splits}

The three sets have three different jobs:

- **Training set:** fit the weights.
- **Validation set (dev set):** make decisions: hyperparameters, architecture, stopping epoch, which checkpoint to keep.
- **Test set:** report a final number, once.

{{figure: week04-splits | The three splits and why the validation set slowly "leaks" into your choices while the test set must be touched only at the end.}}

Why a separate test set? Every time you choose among options based on validation score, you fit the validation set a little. Try 200 hyperparameter configurations and the best validation score is biased upward (selection bias), by an amount that grows with the number of configurations and the noise in the metric. The test set has never influenced any decision, so it gives an unbiased estimate.

Rules for keeping the discipline:

1. **Split once, with a fixed seed, before any exploration**, and store the indices. Never re-split in the middle of a project.
2. **Prevent leakage.** Fit normalization statistics, tokenizers, feature selectors and PCA on the training set only. Group related samples (patients, speakers, videos, near-duplicate images) into the same split. For time series split chronologically.
3. **Test set once.** If you look at it and then change the model, it is a validation set now. Report any number you get from repeated peeking as validation.
4. **Match the deployment distribution** in validation and test, not in training. You may train on synthetic or augmented data, but you evaluate on real, representative data.
5. **Size matters.** A test set of 1,000 examples gives a standard error of about $\sqrt{0.9\cdot0.1/1000}\approx0.9$ percentage points at 90% accuracy. Differences smaller than that are noise; do not report them as improvements.
6. For small datasets use **$k$-fold cross-validation** for the model-selection step, but still hold out a final test set.

### Reproducibility {#reproducibility}

Training is full of randomness: weight initialization, data order, dropout masks, augmentation, GPU non-determinism. You want to be able to rerun the experiment *and* know how much results vary between runs.

- **Seed everything** (Python, NumPy, PyTorch CPU and CUDA) and the `DataLoader` workers.
- **Understand that bitwise reproducibility is expensive.** Some CUDA kernels (atomic adds, cuDNN autotuning) are non-deterministic; `torch.use_deterministic_algorithms(True)` and `torch.backends.cudnn.benchmark=False` help at a speed cost, and results can still differ across GPU models and library versions.
- **Prefer statistical reproducibility.** Run each configuration with 3 to 5 seeds and report mean and standard deviation (or a confidence interval). A difference smaller than the seed-to-seed standard deviation is not a finding.
- **Record the environment:** git commit hash, library versions, hardware, command line, dataset version and split indices. Save the full config with each run.

```python:repro.py
import os, random, json, subprocess, platform
import numpy as np
import torch

def seed_everything(seed: int, deterministic: bool = False):
    random.seed(seed)
    np.random.seed(seed)
    torch.manual_seed(seed)                       # seeds CPU and all CUDA devices
    torch.cuda.manual_seed_all(seed)
    os.environ["PYTHONHASHSEED"] = str(seed)
    if deterministic:
        torch.backends.cudnn.benchmark = False
        torch.use_deterministic_algorithms(True, warn_only=True)

def seed_worker(worker_id):
    """Pass as DataLoader(worker_init_fn=seed_worker, generator=g) so each worker gets a distinct, reproducible seed."""
    s = torch.initial_seed() % 2**32
    np.random.seed(s)
    random.seed(s)

def environment_info():
    try:
        commit = subprocess.check_output(["git", "rev-parse", "HEAD"], stderr=subprocess.DEVNULL).decode().strip()
    except Exception:
        commit = "unknown"
    return {"python": platform.python_version(), "torch": torch.__version__,
            "cuda": torch.version.cuda, "numpy": np.__version__, "git_commit": commit}

seed_everything(0)
print(json.dumps(environment_info(), indent=2))
g = torch.Generator().manual_seed(0)
print(torch.randn(2, generator=g))                # identical on every run
```

### Logging {#logging}

At minimum log, per step or per epoch: train loss, validation loss and metric, learning rate, gradient norm, wall-clock time. Use a tool rather than print statements: TensorBoard (`torch.utils.tensorboard.SummaryWriter`), Weights & Biases, MLflow, or just a CSV or JSON-lines file that you can plot with pandas. The tool matters less than the habit: **every run writes its config and its metrics to its own directory**, so that a month later you can answer "which setting gave that curve?".

Good naming: `runs/2026-10-17_mlp_wd1e-4_do0.3_seed2/`. Keep a short lab notebook (a markdown file) with hypothesis, change, result, interpretation for every experiment. Plot several runs on the same axes; comparisons are what the plots are for.

### Checkpointing {#checkpointing}

A checkpoint should be enough to **resume** training exactly where it stopped, not merely to run inference. That means saving the model state, the optimizer state (Adam moments), the scheduler state, the epoch or step, the RNG states, the best validation metric and the config.

- Save a `last.pt` every epoch (overwrite atomically: write to a temp file and rename) and a `best.pt` whenever validation improves.
- Save `state_dict()`, not the pickled model object, so checkpoints survive code refactors.
- On shared clusters or spot instances jobs get preempted; resume logic is not optional.
- Load with `map_location` so a GPU checkpoint opens on a CPU machine.

```python:checkpoint.py
import os, tempfile
import torch

def save_checkpoint(path, model, opt, sched, epoch, best_val, config):
    state = {
        "model": model.state_dict(), "optimizer": opt.state_dict(),
        "scheduler": sched.state_dict() if sched is not None else None,
        "epoch": epoch, "best_val": best_val, "config": config,
        "rng": {"torch": torch.get_rng_state(),
                "cuda": torch.cuda.get_rng_state_all() if torch.cuda.is_available() else None},
    }
    d = os.path.dirname(path) or "."
    fd, tmp = tempfile.mkstemp(dir=d)
    os.close(fd)
    torch.save(state, tmp)
    os.replace(tmp, path)                          # atomic rename: a crash never leaves a half-written file

def load_checkpoint(path, model, opt=None, sched=None, device="cpu"):
    # weights_only=False because the file contains optimizer/RNG state; only load checkpoints you trust
    ck = torch.load(path, map_location=device, weights_only=False)
    model.load_state_dict(ck["model"])
    if opt is not None:
        opt.load_state_dict(ck["optimizer"])
    if sched is not None and ck["scheduler"] is not None:
        sched.load_state_dict(ck["scheduler"])
    torch.set_rng_state(ck["rng"]["torch"].cpu())
    return ck["epoch"] + 1, ck["best_val"], ck["config"]    # epoch to resume from

if __name__ == "__main__":
    m = torch.nn.Linear(4, 2); o = torch.optim.Adam(m.parameters())
    save_checkpoint("last.pt", m, o, None, epoch=3, best_val=0.42, config={"lr": 1e-3})
    print(load_checkpoint("last.pt", m, o))
```

## Lab: a training pipeline with logging, checkpointing and a regularization ablation {#lab}

In this lab you build one reusable pipeline and use it for a controlled ablation. The task is deliberately small so the experiments run in minutes on a free Colab GPU or even a CPU: classify MNIST digits with an MLP, but train on only **5,000 examples** so that overfitting is easy to provoke. (With the full 60,000 images an MLP barely overfits, which would make the ablation uninformative.)

**Goals.** (1) A config-driven `fit()` with a deterministic split, per-epoch CSV logging, best/last checkpointing, resumption and early stopping. (2) An ablation over regularizers with 3 seeds each. (3) A short report with a table of mean and standard deviation and a paragraph of interpretation.

### Part 1: data and model {#lab-data}

```python:lab_data.py
import numpy as np
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
from torch.utils.data import DataLoader, TensorDataset
from torchvision import datasets

def load_mnist_small(n_train=5000, n_val=5000, seed=0, root="data"):
    """Fixed split: the first n_train indices of a seeded permutation are the training set.
    The official MNIST test set (10,000 images) is kept aside and used only once at the end."""
    tr = datasets.MNIST(root, train=True, download=True)
    te = datasets.MNIST(root, train=False, download=True)
    X = tr.data.float().div(255).view(-1, 784)
    Y = tr.targets
    perm = torch.randperm(len(X), generator=torch.Generator().manual_seed(seed))
    tri, vai = perm[:n_train], perm[n_train:n_train + n_val]
    mean, std = X[tri].mean(), X[tri].std()                    # statistics from the training split only
    norm = lambda a: (a - mean) / std
    Xt = norm(te.data.float().div(255).view(-1, 784))
    return (TensorDataset(norm(X[tri]), Y[tri]), TensorDataset(norm(X[vai]), Y[vai]),
            TensorDataset(Xt, te.targets))

def make_mlp(width=512, depth=3, dropout=0.0):
    layers, d_in = [nn.Flatten()], 784
    for _ in range(depth):
        layers += [nn.Linear(d_in, width), nn.ReLU()]
        if dropout > 0:
            layers.append(nn.Dropout(dropout))
        d_in = width
    layers.append(nn.Linear(d_in, 10))
    return nn.Sequential(*layers)

train_ds, val_ds, test_ds = load_mnist_small()
print(len(train_ds), len(val_ds), len(test_ds))             # 5000 5000 10000
print(make_mlp())
```

### Part 2: the pipeline {#lab-pipeline}

The `fit` function below does everything we discussed: seeding, parameter groups without decay on biases, optional label smoothing and mixup, cosine schedule with warmup, gradient-norm logging, a CSV log, best and last checkpoints, resumption and early stopping.

```python:lab_pipeline.py
import csv, json, math, os, time
import numpy as np
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F
from torch.utils.data import DataLoader

from lab_data import make_mlp, train_ds, val_ds, test_ds
from repro import seed_everything
from checkpoint import save_checkpoint, load_checkpoint
from early_stopping import EarlyStopping

DEFAULT = dict(width=512, depth=3, dropout=0.0, lr=1e-3, weight_decay=0.0, label_smoothing=0.0,
               mixup_alpha=0.0, epochs=60, batch_size=128, patience=10, warmup_epochs=2, seed=0)

@torch.no_grad()
def evaluate(model, loader, device):
    model.eval()                                                  # dropout off, BN in eval mode
    tot_loss, correct, n = 0.0, 0, 0
    for x, y in loader:
        x, y = x.to(device), y.to(device)
        out = model(x)
        tot_loss += F.cross_entropy(out, y, reduction="sum").item()   # plain CE, never smoothed, for comparability
        correct += (out.argmax(1) == y).sum().item()
        n += len(y)
    return tot_loss / n, correct / n

def fit(cfg, run_dir, device=None):
    cfg = {**DEFAULT, **cfg}
    device = device or ("cuda" if torch.cuda.is_available() else "cpu")
    os.makedirs(run_dir, exist_ok=True)
    json.dump(cfg, open(f"{run_dir}/config.json", "w"), indent=2)
    seed_everything(cfg["seed"])

    g = torch.Generator().manual_seed(cfg["seed"])
    train_loader = DataLoader(train_ds, batch_size=cfg["batch_size"], shuffle=True, generator=g)
    val_loader = DataLoader(val_ds, batch_size=1000)

    model = make_mlp(cfg["width"], cfg["depth"], cfg["dropout"]).to(device)
    decay = [p for p in model.parameters() if p.ndim > 1]
    no_decay = [p for p in model.parameters() if p.ndim <= 1]
    opt = torch.optim.AdamW([{"params": decay, "weight_decay": cfg["weight_decay"]},
                             {"params": no_decay, "weight_decay": 0.0}], lr=cfg["lr"])
    steps_per_epoch = len(train_loader)
    total, warm = cfg["epochs"] * steps_per_epoch, cfg["warmup_epochs"] * steps_per_epoch
    lam = lambda s: (s + 1) / warm if s < warm else 0.5 * (1 + math.cos(math.pi * (s - warm) / (total - warm)))
    sched = torch.optim.lr_scheduler.LambdaLR(opt, lam)           # stepped once per iteration

    start_epoch, best_val = 0, float("inf")
    last = f"{run_dir}/last.pt"
    if os.path.exists(last):                                      # resume after a crash or preemption
        start_epoch, best_val, _ = load_checkpoint(last, model, opt, sched, device)
        print(f"resumed from epoch {start_epoch}")
    stopper = EarlyStopping(patience=cfg["patience"], mode="min")
    stopper.best = best_val if best_val < float("inf") else None

    log_path = f"{run_dir}/log.csv"
    new_log = not os.path.exists(log_path)
    f = open(log_path, "a", newline="")
    writer = csv.writer(f)
    if new_log:
        writer.writerow(["epoch", "train_loss", "train_acc", "val_loss", "val_acc", "lr", "grad_norm", "seconds"])

    for epoch in range(start_epoch, cfg["epochs"]):
        model.train()
        t0, run_loss, run_correct, seen, gnorm = time.time(), 0.0, 0, 0, 0.0
        for x, y in train_loader:
            x, y = x.to(device), y.to(device)
            if cfg["mixup_alpha"] > 0:
                lam_mix = float(np.random.beta(cfg["mixup_alpha"], cfg["mixup_alpha"]))
                idx = torch.randperm(x.size(0), device=device)
                xm = lam_mix * x + (1 - lam_mix) * x[idx]
                out = model(xm)
                ls = cfg["label_smoothing"]
                loss = lam_mix * F.cross_entropy(out, y, label_smoothing=ls) + \
                       (1 - lam_mix) * F.cross_entropy(out, y[idx], label_smoothing=ls)
            else:
                out = model(x)
                loss = F.cross_entropy(out, y, label_smoothing=cfg["label_smoothing"])
            opt.zero_grad(set_to_none=True)
            loss.backward()
            gnorm = torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), 5.0).item()   # returns the pre-clip norm
            opt.step()
            sched.step()
            run_loss += loss.item() * len(y)
            run_correct += (out.argmax(1) == y).sum().item()      # meaningless under mixup; reported for reference
            seen += len(y)
        val_loss, val_acc = evaluate(model, val_loader, device)
        writer.writerow([epoch, run_loss / seen, run_correct / seen, val_loss, val_acc,
                         opt.param_groups[0]["lr"], gnorm, time.time() - t0])
        f.flush()
        if val_loss < best_val:
            best_val = val_loss
            save_checkpoint(f"{run_dir}/best.pt", model, opt, sched, epoch, best_val, cfg)
        save_checkpoint(last, model, opt, sched, epoch, best_val, cfg)
        if stopper.step(val_loss, model):
            print(f"early stop at epoch {epoch}")
            break
    f.close()

    load_checkpoint(f"{run_dir}/best.pt", model, device=device)      # evaluate the best checkpoint, not the last weights
    val_loss, val_acc = evaluate(model, val_loader, device)
    return {"val_loss": val_loss, "val_acc": val_acc, "best_epoch": torch.load(f"{run_dir}/best.pt", weights_only=False)["epoch"]}

if __name__ == "__main__":
    print(fit({"epochs": 5}, "runs/smoke"))
```

Things to notice: the validation loss is always plain cross-entropy even when training uses label smoothing, so numbers are comparable across runs; the scheduler steps per iteration; we restore the best checkpoint before the final evaluation; the test set is not touched anywhere in `fit`.

## Common mistakes and debugging tips {#common-mistakes}

Beyond the [bug table](#common-bugs), these are the mistakes that most often waste a week.

- **Tuning on the test set (even implicitly).** Looking at test accuracy after every experiment turns it into a validation set. Symptom: your reported number is higher than any held-out data will ever give you. Fix: lock the test set away until the very end.
- **Judging regularization by training loss.** Label smoothing and mixup raise training loss by design; dropout makes it noisy. Always compare validation metrics measured in eval mode.
- **Weight decay that does nothing, or too much.** With a typical AdamW setup, $\lambda=0$ versus $10^{-6}$ is indistinguishable; $\lambda=1$ collapses the weights. Search $\lambda$ over several orders of magnitude, and remember that the effective strength depends on the learning rate.
- **Using Adam's `weight_decay` argument and expecting AdamW behavior.** Adam adds the penalty to the gradient (coupled); AdamW decouples. They behave differently.
- **Dropout in the wrong place.** On the output layer, directly before the loss, or stacked with BatchNorm without thought. Symptom: noisy validation or train/eval mismatch.
- **Comparing runs with different seeds and concluding too much.** A 0.3% accuracy difference on a 5,000-example validation set with one seed is noise.
- **Changing several things at once.** Then you cannot tell which change mattered. One change per experiment, with the ablation logged.
- **Early stopping on a noisy metric with patience 1.** You will stop in the first dip. Use patience of 5 to 20 evaluations.
- **A leaked split.** Duplicates, near-duplicates, or the same subject in train and validation. Symptom: validation accuracy far above what the task should allow. Fix: deduplicate and split by group.
- **Resuming without optimizer state.** The Adam moments restart at zero and the loss jumps. Save and load the optimizer and scheduler.
- **Forgetting that augmentation changes the effective epoch.** Strong augmentation needs 2 to 10 times more epochs; a fixed epoch budget can make a regularized model look worse.

## Exercises {#exercises}

:::exercise Exercise 1: bias-variance by hand (derivation)
A model predicts a constant $\hat f = c$ fitted as the mean of $n$ noisy observations $y_i = g + \varepsilon_i$ of a single target value $g$ (so the "true function" is constant), with $\Var(\varepsilon)=\sigma^2$. Compute bias$^2$, variance and expected squared test error. Now **shrink** the estimator: $\hat f_\alpha = \alpha\,\bar y$ with $0<\alpha<1$. Show that for some $\alpha<1$ the expected test error is lower than for $\alpha=1$, and find the best $\alpha$.

:::details Solution
For $\hat f=\bar y$: $\E[\bar y] = g$, so bias $=0$; $\Var(\bar y) = \sigma^2/n$; expected test error $= 0 + \sigma^2/n + \sigma^2$.

For $\hat f_\alpha=\alpha\bar y$: mean $\alpha g$, bias$^2=(1-\alpha)^2g^2$, variance $\alpha^2\sigma^2/n$. Expected error $= (1-\alpha)^2 g^2 + \alpha^2\sigma^2/n + \sigma^2$. Differentiate in $\alpha$: $-2(1-\alpha)g^2 + 2\alpha\sigma^2/n = 0$, so
$$\alpha^\star = \frac{g^2}{g^2+\sigma^2/n}<1.$$
At $\alpha=1$ the derivative is $2\sigma^2/n>0$, so decreasing $\alpha$ slightly lowers the error: introducing a little bias buys a larger reduction in variance. This is the James-Stein/ridge idea in one dimension. (The optimal $\alpha$ depends on the unknown $g$, which is why practical regularization strength is chosen by validation.)
:::
:::

:::exercise Exercise 2: ridge as MAP (derivation)
Linear regression with $y = \boldsymbol{w}^\top\boldsymbol{x} + \varepsilon$, $\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)$ and prior $\boldsymbol{w}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol 0,\tau^2\mathbf{I})$. (a) Show that the MAP estimate minimizes $\norm{\boldsymbol y - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol w}^2 + \lambda\norm{\boldsymbol w}^2$ and give $\lambda$. (b) Give the closed-form solution.

:::details Solution
(a) $-\log p(\boldsymbol w\mid\text{data}) = \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\norm{\boldsymbol y-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol w}^2 + \frac{1}{2\tau^2}\norm{\boldsymbol w}^2 + \text{const}$. Multiply by $2\sigma^2$: minimize $\norm{\boldsymbol y-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol w}^2 + \frac{\sigma^2}{\tau^2}\norm{\boldsymbol w}^2$, so $\lambda=\sigma^2/\tau^2$ (more noise or a tighter prior means more regularization).

(b) Set the gradient to zero: $-2\mathbf{X}^\top(\boldsymbol y-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol w)+2\lambda\boldsymbol w=0$, so $\hat{\boldsymbol w}=(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}+\lambda\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\boldsymbol y$. The matrix is always invertible for $\lambda>0$, which is also why ridge cures the ill-conditioning behind the interpolation peak.
:::
:::

:::exercise Exercise 3: soft-thresholding (derivation)
Derive the minimizer of $\frac12(w-z)^2+\lambda|w|$ and show it is the soft-threshold $\operatorname{sign}(z)\max(|z|-\lambda,0)$.

:::details Solution
The objective is convex. For $w>0$ the derivative is $w - z + \lambda$, zero at $w=z-\lambda$, valid if $z>\lambda$. For $w<0$ the derivative is $w-z-\lambda$, zero at $w=z+\lambda$, valid if $z<-\lambda$. If $|z|\le\lambda$, then the subdifferential at $0$ is $-z+\lambda[-1,1]$, which contains $0$ exactly when $|z|\le\lambda$, so $w^\star=0$. Combining the three cases gives the soft-threshold.
:::
:::

:::exercise Exercise 4: inverted dropout (derivation and coding)
(a) Show that for inverted dropout on a unit $h$ with drop probability $p$ the mean is $h$ and the variance is $\frac{p}{1-p}h^2$. (b) Suppose you forget to call `model.eval()` at test time for a network with dropout. What happens to the outputs, and how would you notice? (c) A colleague uses `nn.Dropout(0.5)` but implements it by hand *without* the $1/(1-p)$ factor and also does not rescale at test time. What error does a linear output layer see?

:::details Solution
(a) $\tilde h = h\,m/(1-p)$ with $m\sim\text{Bernoulli}(1-p)$: $\E\tilde h = h(1-p)/(1-p)=h$. $\E[\tilde h^2]=h^2(1-p)/(1-p)^2=h^2/(1-p)$, so $\Var=h^2/(1-p)-h^2=h^2\,p/(1-p)$.

(b) Dropout stays active, so predictions are random: the same input gives different outputs on repeated calls, validation metrics are noisy and lower than they should be (more so with larger $p$). You would notice by evaluating the same batch twice and comparing, or by asserting `not model.training`.

(c) At training time the layer receives inputs scaled by $(1-p)=0.5$ in expectation, at test time by 1, so pre-activations are twice as large as anything seen in training. For a linear layer the test outputs are $1/(1-p)$ times the training-time mean (before bias), a systematic miscalibration. Inverted scaling avoids this.
:::
:::

:::exercise Exercise 5: label smoothing (derivation and coding)
(a) For $K$ classes and smoothing $\varepsilon$, what is the minimum achievable cross-entropy and at which predicted distribution? (b) Implement label smoothing yourself and check against `F.cross_entropy(..., label_smoothing=eps)`. Note the exact definition PyTorch uses.

:::details Solution
(a) Cross-entropy $H(\tilde y,p)=H(\tilde y)+\KL(\tilde y\,\|\,p)$ is minimized at $p=\tilde y$ and the minimum is the entropy of the smoothed target: $H(\tilde y) = -a\log a-(K-1)b\log b$ with $a=1-\varepsilon+\varepsilon/K$ and $b=\varepsilon/K$. For $\varepsilon=0.1$, $K=10$: $a=0.91$, $b=0.01$, $H\approx 0.91\cdot0.0943+9\cdot0.01\cdot4.605\approx0.086+0.414=0.50$ nats. So the training loss can never go below about 0.5 here, which is why you must not compare training losses between runs with different $\varepsilon$.

(b) See `smoothed_ce` in the [label-smoothing section](#label-smoothing); PyTorch's definition is $(1-\varepsilon)\,\text{CE}(\text{hard}) + \varepsilon\cdot\text{CE}(\text{uniform})$, identical to ours, so the two printed values agree to floating-point precision.
:::
:::

:::exercise Exercise 6: weight decay with Adam (conceptual, challenging)
Consider a single scalar weight $w$ with loss gradient $g_t$ and Adam's normalized step $\hat m_t/(\sqrt{\hat v_t}+\epsilon)$. (a) Write the update for Adam with an L2 penalty $\frac{\lambda}{2}w^2$ added to the loss, and for AdamW with decoupled decay. (b) Two weights have loss-gradients of typical magnitude $10^{-1}$ and $10^{-4}$. In the L2-Adam version, roughly how strongly is each one decayed relative to the other? (c) Which version behaves like "shrink all weights by the same factor"?

:::details Solution
(a) Adam+L2: the gradient is $g_t+\lambda w_t$, so $w_{t+1}=w_t-\eta\,\dfrac{\hat m_t}{\sqrt{\hat v_t}+\epsilon}$ with $m,v$ built from $g_t+\lambda w_t$. AdamW: $w_{t+1}=w_t-\eta\,\dfrac{\hat m_t}{\sqrt{\hat v_t}+\epsilon}-\eta\lambda w_t$ with $m,v$ built from $g_t$ alone.

(b) In Adam+L2, if $\lambda w$ is small compared with $g$, the denominator is about $|g|$, so the decay part of the step is about $\eta\lambda w/|g|$. The weight with gradient scale $10^{-4}$ is decayed about $10^{3}$ times more strongly than the one with scale $10^{-1}$ (for equal $w$). If $\lambda w$ dominates, both get a normalized step of size about $\eta$ regardless of $w$, which is also not proportional shrinkage.

(c) AdamW: each step multiplies every weight by $(1-\eta\lambda)$ independent of gradient history.
:::
:::

:::exercise Exercise 7: the Beta distribution in mixup (coding)
Sample $\lambda\sim\text{Beta}(\alpha,\alpha)$ for $\alpha\in\{0.1,0.4,1,4\}$ with NumPy. For each, report $\Pr[\min(\lambda,1-\lambda)<0.05]$ (how often a "mixed" example is nearly clean). Explain the consequence for the strength of regularization. Then give the expected CutMix box area fraction for $\alpha=1$.

:::details Solution
```python
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
for a in [0.1, 0.4, 1, 4]:
    lam = rng.beta(a, a, 200000)
    print(a, np.mean(np.minimum(lam, 1 - lam) < 0.05))
```
Roughly $0.75$ for $\alpha=0.1$, $0.36$ for $0.4$, $0.10$ for $1$ and about $0.0005$ for $4$ (exact values for $\alpha=1$: $\Pr=0.1$, since $\lambda$ is uniform). Small $\alpha$ means most examples are almost unmixed, so the regularization is mild; large $\alpha$ means almost every example is a strong mixture. For CutMix the pasted-area fraction is $1-\lambda$, whose expectation under $\text{Beta}(1,1)$ is $0.5$ (before clipping at image borders, which slightly reduces it).

:::exercise Exercise 8: random versus grid search (what would happen if...)
You have 16 trials. The score depends only on the learning rate (a smooth bump with width 0.5 in log10 units); the other three hyperparameters are irrelevant. (a) With 16 trials spread over a full grid on the 4 hyperparameters, how many distinct learning rates are tested? (b) How many does random search test? (c) If the best log10-LR region occupies 10% of the range, what is the probability that random search with 16 trials lands at least one trial in it? Compare with a grid.

:::details Solution
(a) A full grid with $16=2^4$ trials gives only 2 values per hyperparameter, hence 2 distinct learning rates (a 4-point grid on each of 4 hyperparameters would take 256 trials). (b) Random search tests 16 distinct learning rates. (c) $1-0.9^{16}\approx0.81$. A 2-point grid hits the 10% region only if its fixed points happen to fall inside it, and with 2 points that is unlikely ($\le 20\%$ for a random placement). Random search degrades gracefully as the number of irrelevant hyperparameters grows; grid search does not.
:::

:::exercise Exercise 9: design a leak-free pipeline (conceptual, challenging)
You have 100,000 chest X-rays from 20,000 patients (about 5 per patient), with a rare disease. You plan: random 80/10/10 split of images, standardize with the dataset mean and std, use 5-fold CV to tune, and report the best CV score. List every flaw and give a corrected protocol.

:::details Solution
Flaws: (1) the random image-level split puts images of the same patient in train and test, so the model can identify patients, not disease (group leakage). (2) Mean and std were computed on all data, leaking test statistics (mild, but a pattern that becomes serious with PCA or feature selection). (3) Reporting the best CV score is selection bias; CV is a validation tool and the maximum over many configs is optimistic. (4) A rare disease needs stratification and a metric beyond accuracy (AUROC or AUPRC), and confidence intervals.

Corrected protocol: split by patient ID into train/validation/test, stratified on the label as far as possible; compute normalization on training patients only; tune with grouped $k$-fold CV inside the training portion (`GroupKFold`); freeze the configuration; evaluate once on the held-out test patients and report a metric with a bootstrap confidence interval; additionally examine an external site if available.
:::

:::exercise Exercise 10: debugging case studies (what would happen if...)
For each symptom, name the two most likely causes and the first check you would run: (a) training loss is exactly $2.3026$ for 10 classes after 10 epochs; (b) training accuracy 99%, validation accuracy 11%; (c) the loss becomes `NaN` at step 1,200; (d) validation loss is lower than training loss at every epoch, with dropout 0.5 and heavy augmentation.

:::details Solution
(a) Nothing is learning: learning rate effectively zero or scheduler stuck in warmup; dead network (all ReLUs dead from bad init or too-large first step), or the graph is detached. Check: gradient norms and the overfit-one-batch test.
(b) Train/eval mismatch: different normalization or preprocessing in the validation pipeline, or BatchNorm/dropout mode wrong; or labels misaligned in the validation loader (for example shuffled separately from the data). ## Key takeaways

:::key Key takeaways
- Squared error splits into bias$^2$ + variance + irreducible noise. Regularization trades a little bias for a lot less variance; the training loss alone cannot tell you the right trade.
- Double descent shows that test error can fall again past the interpolation threshold. It does not remove the need for regularization: weight decay, augmentation and label smoothing smooth the peak.
- L2 is a Gaussian prior and shrinks weights proportionally; L1 is a Laplace prior and produces exact zeros through its corners. With Adam use decoupled weight decay (AdamW) and skip biases and norm parameters.
- Dropout multiplies activations by a random mask (inverted scaling keeps the expectation unchanged); it approximates an ensemble of thinned networks. Always switch it off with `model.eval()`.
- Label smoothing caps the logit gap; mixup and CutMix train the model to behave linearly between examples; early stopping and augmentation are among the cheapest and most reliable regularizers.
- Search hyperparameters on log scales with random search and successive halving; use Bayesian optimization when runs are expensive and few; use an LR range test to find the learning rate first.
- Debug in order: look at the data, check the initial loss ($\approx\ln K$), overfit a single batch, then scale up one change at a time; read loss curves as diagnoses.
- Reproducible experiments mean fixed splits, seeded runs with several seeds, logged configs and metrics, resumable checkpoints and a test set touched exactly once.
:::
