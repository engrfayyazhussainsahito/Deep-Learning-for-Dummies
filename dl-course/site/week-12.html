Every model you have seen so far answers a discriminative question: given xxx, what is yyy? A generative model answers a different one: what does the data itself look like? It learns a distribution pθ(x)p_\theta(x)pθ​(x) close to the true data distribution pdata(x)p_\text{data}(x)pdata​(x) so that we can draw new samples (images, molecules, audio), evaluate how plausible an input is, learn compact representations, or fill in missing parts. Week 11 used self-supervision to learn features. This week and the next use the data's own structure to learn the distribution.

The difficulty is geometric. An image with 64×64×3=12,28864\times64\times3=12{,}28864×64×3=12,288 pixels lives in a space of enormous dimension, yet natural images occupy a tiny, curved, low-dimensional sliver of it (the manifold hypothesis). A generative model must find that sliver. The two families in this lesson attack the problem differently. Variational autoencoders write down a latent-variable model and maximize a tractable lower bound on the likelihood. Generative adversarial networks give up on the likelihood altogether and train a generator to fool a learned critic. Week 13 will add diffusion models, which now dominate image generation, and the ideas here (latent spaces, KL divergences, adversarial losses, perceptual metrics) reappear there.

A map of generative models

The first question to ask of any generative model is what can you compute with it?

  • Explicit, tractable density. You can evaluate pθ(x)p_\theta(x)pθ​(x) exactly. Autoregressive models factor p(x)=∏ip(xi∣x<i)p(x)=\prod_i p(x_i\mid x_{<i})p(x)=∏i​p(xi​∣x<i​) (PixelCNN, the GPT family of Week 10). Normalizing flows push a simple density through an invertible map and track the change of variables. Exact likelihood is wonderful for model comparison, but the architectural constraints (ordering, invertibility) are costly, and autoregressive sampling is sequential.
  • Explicit, approximate density. The model defines a density but you only evaluate a bound or an unnormalized value. VAEs maximize the ELBO. Energy-based models define p(x)∝e−Eθ(x)p(x)\propto e^{-E_\theta(x)}p(x)∝e−Eθ​(x) with an intractable normalizer.
  • Implicit density. There is a sampler x=Gθ(z)x=G_\theta(z)x=Gθ​(z) but no way to evaluate pθ(x)p_\theta(x)pθ​(x). GANs are the main example. Sampling is a single forward pass, which is fast, but there is no likelihood to monitor and no guarantee that every part of the data is covered.
  • Iterative and score-based models. Diffusion models (Week 13) learn to reverse a gradual noising process. They optimize a variational bound (so they are close cousins of VAEs) but generate by many small steps (so they behave like samplers).

Three desiderata pull against each other: sample quality, mode coverage and likelihood evaluation, and sampling speed. For several years the "generative trilemma" was a fair summary: GANs gave fast, sharp samples but poor coverage and unstable training; VAEs gave good coverage and stable training but blurry samples; autoregressive models gave exact likelihoods but slow sampling. Diffusion improved all three at the cost of speed. Knowing which axis a method sacrifices lets you choose sensibly.

A short history

Restricted Boltzmann machines and deep belief nets (Hinton, Osindero and Teh, 2006) were the first modern deep generative models; Hinton and Salakhutdinov (Science, 2006) used deep autoencoders for dimensionality reduction. Denoising autoencoders (Vincent et al., 2008) tied representation learning to a noise-removal task. In 2013 and 2014, Kingma and Welling (and, independently, Rezende, Mohamed and Wierstra) introduced the VAE, and Goodfellow and colleagues introduced the GAN. From 2015 to 2019 GAN research moved fast: DCGAN (2015), Wasserstein GAN (2017), progressive growing (2018), BigGAN (2018) and StyleGAN (2018 and 2019), whose faces became the symbol of the field's progress.

Autoencoders

An autoencoder (AE) is a network trained to copy its input to its output through a narrow channel. It has an encoder fϕ:Rd→Rkf_\phi:\R^d\to\R^kfϕ​:Rd→Rk that maps xxx to a code z=fϕ(x)z=f_\phi(x)z=fϕ​(x) and a decoder gθ:Rk→Rdg_\theta:\R^k\to\R^dgθ​:Rk→Rd that returns a reconstruction x^=gθ(z)\hat x=g_\theta(z)x^=gθ​(z). Training minimizes a reconstruction loss, usually squared error for real-valued data or binary cross-entropy for pixels in [0,1][0,1][0,1]:

LAE(ϕ,θ)=1N∑n=1N∥xn−gθ(fϕ(xn))∥2.\mathcal{L}_\text{AE}(\phi,\theta)=\frac1N\sum_{n=1}^N \norm{x_n-g_\theta(f_\phi(x_n))}^2 .LAE​(ϕ,θ)=N1​n=1∑N​∥xn​−gθ​(fϕ​(xn​))∥2.

Copying is trivial unless the network is prevented from learning the identity function. Every variant of the autoencoder is a different way of preventing this, and the regularizer shapes what the code means.

Undercomplete autoencoders and PCA

The simplest constraint is a bottleneck: choose k<dk<dk<d so the code cannot hold everything. With a linear encoder and decoder and squared error, the optimum has a closed form. The decoder spans the top-kkk principal subspace, so a linear autoencoder learns exactly the same subspace as principal component analysis (Baldi and Hornik, 1989), although not necessarily the same basis within it. The reconstruction error is the sum of the discarded eigenvalues of the data covariance. The chart shows this on the 8×88\times88×8 digits dataset from scikit-learn (1,797 images, d=64d=64d=64): the error falls quickly because digits are highly redundant.

With nonlinear layers (convolutional or MLP encoders and decoders), the autoencoder can follow a curved manifold rather than a flat subspace, which is why a deep autoencoder with k=2k=2k=2 separates MNIST digits far better than PCA with k=2k=2k=2. Hinton and Salakhutdinov made that point in 2006.

import torch, torch.nn as nn, torch.nn.functional as F

class AutoEncoder(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, d=784, k=32, hidden=256):
        super().__init__()
        self.enc = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(d, hidden), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(hidden, k))
        self.dec = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(k, hidden), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(hidden, d))   # logits for pixels in [0, 1]

    def forward(self, x):
        z = self.enc(x)                       # (B, k) the code
        return self.dec(z), z                 # (B, d) logits, (B, k)

model = AutoEncoder()
x = torch.rand(16, 784)                       # stand-in for a batch of flattened 28x28 images
logits, z = model(x)
loss = F.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(logits, x)    # per-pixel Bernoulli reconstruction loss
print(logits.shape, z.shape, loss.item())               # torch.Size([16, 784]) torch.Size([16, 32])

Denoising autoencoders

A denoising autoencoder (Vincent, Larochelle, Bengio and Manzagol, ICML 2008) corrupts the input, x~∼q(x~∣x)\tilde x\sim q(\tilde x\mid x)x~∼q(x~∣x) (Gaussian noise, random pixel dropout, or masking), and trains the network to reconstruct the clean xxx:

LDAE=Ex∼pdata Ex~∼q(⋅∣x)∥x−gθ(fϕ(x~))∥2.\mathcal{L}_\text{DAE}=\E_{x\sim p_\text{data}}\,\E_{\tilde x\sim q(\cdot\mid x)}\norm{x-g_\theta(f_\phi(\tilde x))}^2 .LDAE​=Ex∼pdata​​Ex~∼q(⋅∣x)​∥x−gθ​(fϕ​(x~))∥2.

Because the identity map no longer reduces the loss, the network must learn the structure of the data: which pixel patterns are plausible, and therefore which direction leads back to the manifold. A known result (Vincent, 2011; Alain and Bengio, 2014) says that for small Gaussian corruption x~=x+σϵ\tilde x=x+\sigma\epsilonx~=x+σϵ, the optimal denoiser satisfies

g∗(x~)−x~  ≈  σ2 ∇x~log⁡pσ(x~),g^*(\tilde x)-\tilde x\;\approx\;\sigma^2\,\nabla_{\tilde x}\log p_\sigma(\tilde x),g∗(x~)−x~≈σ2∇x~​logpσ​(x~),

i.e. the reconstruction residual points along the score of the (noised) data density. This connection is the seed of score-based and diffusion models in Week 13, and the masked autoencoder of Week 11 is a denoising autoencoder whose "noise" is the deletion of 75% of the patches. Note that a denoising autoencoder can have a code larger than the input; the corruption alone does the regularizing.

pythondenoising_sparse.py
import torch, torch.nn as nn, torch.nn.functional as F

def denoising_step(model, x, opt, sigma=0.3):
    x_noisy = (x + sigma * torch.randn_like(x)).clamp(0, 1)      # corrupt the input
    logits, _ = model(x_noisy)
    loss = F.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(logits, x)          # but reconstruct the CLEAN target
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
    return loss.item()

def sparse_loss(logits, x, z_act, l1=1e-3):
    """Sparse autoencoder: reconstruction + L1 penalty on the (non-negative) code activations."""
    rec = F.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(logits, x)
    return rec + l1 * z_act.abs().mean()

class SparseAE(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, d=784, k=512):                             # overcomplete: k > d is fine when the code is sparse
        super().__init__()
        self.enc, self.dec = nn.Linear(d, k), nn.Linear(k, d)
    def forward(self, x):
        a = F.relu(self.enc(x))                                   # (B, k) non-negative code
        return self.dec(a), a

m = SparseAE(); x = torch.rand(32, 784)
logits, a = m(x)
print(sparse_loss(logits, x, a).item(), (a > 0).float().mean().item())   # fraction of active units

Sparse and contractive autoencoders

A sparse autoencoder may have more code units than inputs (k>dk>dk>d) but penalizes activity, either with an L1L_1L1​ term λ∥z∥1\lambda\norm{z}_1λ∥z∥1​ (as in the code above), or with a KL penalty that pushes each unit's average activation toward a small target ρ\rhoρ, or with a hard top-kkk rule that keeps only the kkk largest activations (Makhzani and Frey, 2013, "k-Sparse Autoencoders"). The code then behaves like a learned dictionary in which each input is explained by a few active atoms; individual units tend to become interpretable detectors. This idea has recently returned in the interpretability of large language models, where sparse autoencoders are trained on a model's hidden activations to find human-readable features. A contractive autoencoder (Rifai et al., 2011) penalizes the Frobenius norm of the encoder's Jacobian, ∥∂fϕ/∂x∥F2\norm{\partial f_\phi/\partial x}_F^2∥∂fϕ​/∂x∥F2​, making the code insensitive to small input changes.

Why an autoencoder is not a generative model

If you sample zzz from some distribution and decode it, you will usually get garbage. The encoder maps the data to arbitrary islands in code space, with unused gaps between them and no rule for what the gaps mean. We have no distribution over zzz to sample from, and decoding a point between two islands has no reason to produce a plausible image. A fix that comes to mind is to fit a Gaussian mixture to the codes after training. That works somewhat (and is a legitimate baseline), but the VAE builds the answer directly into training: it forces the code distribution to be close to a known prior so that sampling from the prior is meaningful.

Variational autoencoders

The latent-variable model

A VAE (Kingma and Welling, ICLR 2014, "Auto-Encoding Variational Bayes") posits a simple generative story:

  1. Draw a latent code z∼p(z)=N(0,I)z\sim p(z)=\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I})z∼p(z)=N(0,I) with z∈Rkz\in\R^kz∈Rk.
  2. Draw the data x∼pθ(x∣z)x\sim p_\theta(x\mid z)x∼pθ​(x∣z), where the parameters of this conditional are produced by a neural network (the decoder): a Bernoulli over pixels with logits gθ(z)g_\theta(z)gθ​(z), or a Gaussian with mean gθ(z)g_\theta(z)gθ​(z) and fixed variance.

The marginal likelihood is pθ(x)=∫pθ(x∣z)p(z) dzp_\theta(x)=\int p_\theta(x\mid z)p(z)\,dzpθ​(x)=∫pθ​(x∣z)p(z)dz, an integral over all latent codes. For a neural-network decoder it has no closed form, and a naive Monte Carlo estimate 1S∑spθ(x∣zs)\frac1S\sum_s p_\theta(x\mid z_s)S1​∑s​pθ​(x∣zs​) with zs∼p(z)z_s\sim p(z)zs​∼p(z) is hopeless in high dimension: almost every random zzz explains xxx terribly, so the estimator is dominated by rare lucky samples. The posterior pθ(z∣x)=pθ(x∣z)p(z)/pθ(x)p_\theta(z\mid x)=p_\theta(x\mid z)p(z)/p_\theta(x)pθ​(z∣x)=pθ​(x∣z)p(z)/pθ​(x), which would tell us which codes are plausible for a given xxx, is intractable for the same reason.

The variational trick is to introduce an approximate posterior qϕ(z∣x)q_\phi(z\mid x)qϕ​(z∣x) (the encoder), usually a diagonal Gaussian N(μϕ(x),diag⁡(σϕ2(x)))\mathcal{N}(\mu_\phi(x),\diag(\sigma_\phi^2(x)))N(μϕ​(x),diag(σϕ2​(x))), and to optimize a lower bound on log⁡pθ(x)\log p_\theta(x)logpθ​(x) that involves qϕq_\phiqϕ​.

Derivation 2: the gap is a KL divergence

Jensen's route shows the inequality but not how loose it is. Start instead from the true posterior. For a fixed θ\thetaθ, maximizing the ELBO over ϕ\phiϕ is the same as minimizing the KL from the approximate to the true posterior. Over θ\thetaθ, it pushes up a lower bound on the log-likelihood. The gap is the quality of the encoder, and we can never evaluate it, because it involves the posterior we cannot compute.

Read the two ELBO terms as a compression trade-off. The reconstruction term asks the code to carry enough information about xxx for the decoder to rebuild it. The KL term charges the encoder for every nat of information it puts in the code beyond what the prior already expects, and keeps the codes of different inputs overlapping and centred, so that a draw from p(z)p(z)p(z) falls in a region the decoder has seen. A VAE is therefore an autoencoder with a noise injected in the code (the variance of qqq) and a penalty for deviating from the prior.

Another view: what does the average KL measure?

Averaged over the data, ExDKL(qϕ(z∣x)∥p(z))=Iq(x;z)+DKL(qϕ(z)∥p(z))\E_x\KL(q_\phi(z\mid x)\|p(z))=I_q(x;z)+\KL(q_\phi(z)\|p(z))Ex​DKL​(qϕ​(z∣x)∥p(z))=Iq​(x;z)+DKL​(qϕ​(z)∥p(z)) where qϕ(z)=Exqϕ(z∣x)q_\phi(z)=\E_x q_\phi(z\mid x)qϕ​(z)=Ex​qϕ​(z∣x) is the aggregate posterior and IqI_qIq​ is the mutual information between data and code under the encoder. The reparameterization trick

To maximize the ELBO by gradient ascent, we need ∇ϕEqϕ(z∣x)[log⁡pθ(x∣z)]\nabla_\phi\E_{q_\phi(z\mid x)}[\log p_\theta(x\mid z)]∇ϕ​Eqϕ​(z∣x)​[logpθ​(x∣z)]. The expectation depends on ϕ\phiϕ through the distribution being sampled from, and a sample z∼qϕz\sim q_\phiz∼qϕ​ is a random draw: backpropagation cannot pass through "sampling". The generic score-function (REINFORCE) estimator works, but has high variance.

The reparameterization trick rewrites the sample as a deterministic, differentiable function of the parameters and an independent noise variable:

Why the noise matters

Imagine the encoder wants to be accurate, so it makes σ\sigmaσ tiny. Then the code is a deterministic point, the KL term −log⁡σ2-\log\sigma^2−logσ2 becomes huge, and the model pays heavily. If instead it uses a large σ\sigmaσ, the decoder sees a smeared code and reconstructs poorly. The optimum balances these. The effect is that nearby points in latent space decode to similar images, since the decoder has been trained on a cloud around each code, not on a single point. This smoothness is what makes interpolation and sampling work.

import torch, torch.nn as nn, torch.nn.functional as F

class VAE(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, d=784, hidden=400, k=20):
        super().__init__()
        self.fc1 = nn.Linear(d, hidden)
        self.fc_mu = nn.Linear(hidden, k)
        self.fc_logvar = nn.Linear(hidden, k)          # predict log sigma^2: unconstrained and numerically stable
        self.fc3 = nn.Linear(k, hidden)
        self.fc4 = nn.Linear(hidden, d)

    def encode(self, x):
        h = F.relu(self.fc1(x))
        return self.fc_mu(h), self.fc_logvar(h)        # each (B, k)

    def reparameterize(self, mu, logvar):
        std = torch.exp(0.5 * logvar)                  # sigma = exp(logvar / 2)
        eps = torch.randn_like(std)                    # noise independent of the parameters
        return mu + std * eps                          # (B, k)

    def decode(self, z):
        return self.fc4(F.relu(self.fc3(z)))           # (B, d) logits

    def forward(self, x):
        mu, logvar = self.encode(x)
        z = self.reparameterize(mu, logvar)
        return self.decode(z), mu, logvar

def vae_loss(logits, x, mu, logvar, beta=1.0):
    """Negative ELBO summed over pixels / latent dims, averaged over the batch."""
    rec = F.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(logits, x, reduction="none").sum(dim=1)     # (B,)
    kl = 0.5 * (mu.pow(2) + logvar.exp() - 1 - logvar).sum(dim=1)                        # (B,)
    return (rec + beta * kl).mean(), rec.mean(), kl.mean()

model = VAE()
x = torch.rand(64, 784)
logits, mu, logvar = model(x)
loss, rec, kl = vae_loss(logits, x, mu, logvar)
print(logits.shape, mu.shape, loss.item(), rec.item(), kl.item())
Sum, do not average, over pixels and latent dimensions

The ELBO is a sum over pixels (the log-likelihood of the whole image) and a sum over latent dimensions. If you average over pixels with reduction="mean" but sum the KL, you have silently multiplied the KL weight by the number of pixels (784 on MNIST). The result is an over-regularized VAE with blurry outputs and a collapsed code. If you do use means, choose the weight explicitly as a β\betaβ.

Beta-VAE and the rate-distortion trade-off

Higgins et al. (ICLR 2017) proposed β\betaβ-VAE, which multiplies the KL term:

Lβ=Eqϕ(z∣x)[log⁡pθ(x∣z)]−β DKL(qϕ(z∣x) ∥ p(z)).\mathcal{L}_\beta=\E_{q_\phi(z\mid x)}[\log p_\theta(x\mid z)]-\beta\,\KL\big(q_\phi(z\mid x)\,\|\,p(z)\big).Lβ​=Eqϕ​(z∣x)​[logpθ​(x∣z)]−βDKL​(qϕ​(z∣x)∥p(z)).

With β>1\beta>1β>1 the model is pushed to use less information, and to align the code with the factorized prior, which empirically encourages disentangled factors (one latent per factor of variation such as object size, rotation or x-position) on simple synthetic data. Burgess et al. (2018) argued for increasing the capacity target gradually. The price is worse reconstructions. With β<1\beta<1β<1 you recover sharper reconstructions but a code that departs from the prior and is a poor sampler. The cleanest view is rate and distortion: the KL is the rate (nats to describe the code), the reconstruction error is the distortion, and β\betaβ is a Lagrange multiplier selecting a point on the optimal rate-distortion curve. Posterior collapse

Sometimes the KL term wins completely: qϕ(z∣x)≈p(z)q_\phi(z\mid x)\approx p(z)qϕ​(z∣x)≈p(z) for every xxx, the code carries no information, and the decoder ignores zzz and models the average data (a blur for images, a language model for text). This is posterior collapse, and you can see it at β=16\beta=16β=16 in the chart above and in the demo. It is most common when the decoder is powerful enough to model the data without the latent (an autoregressive decoder over pixels or words, as in the VAE language models of Bowman et al., 2016), when the KL weight is too large relative to the reconstruction scale, and early in training, when the encoder is still uninformative.

Standard remedies:

  • KL annealing: warm up β\betaβ from 0 to 1 over the first epochs (Bowman et al., 2016), so the decoder first learns to use zzz.
  • Free bits (Kingma et al., 2016): ignore the KL of a latent dimension until it exceeds a threshold, ∑jmax⁡(λ,KLj)\sum_j\max(\lambda,\mathrm{KL}_j)∑j​max(λ,KLj​).
  • Weaken the decoder (smaller receptive field, dropout on inputs) or strengthen the encoder, so information in zzz is useful.
  • Lower-capacity or discrete latents (VQ-VAE), which sidesteps the issue because there is no KL term to win.

How to detect it: the KL per latent dimension (or the variance of μj\mu_jμj​ across the data) is near zero for most dimensions, and the "active units" count (dimensions with Varx[μj(x)]>ϵ\mathrm{Var}_x[\mu_j(x)]>\epsilonVarx​[μj​(x)]>ϵ) is small. Always log the reconstruction and the KL separately, not just their sum.

import torch, torch.nn as nn, torch.nn.functional as F

class VectorQuantizer(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, num_codes=512, dim=64, beta=0.25):
        super().__init__()
        self.codebook = nn.Embedding(num_codes, dim)
        self.codebook.weight.data.uniform_(-1 / num_codes, 1 / num_codes)
        self.beta = beta

    def forward(self, z_e):                                    # z_e: (B, D, H, W) encoder output
        B, D, H, W = z_e.shape
        flat = z_e.permute(0, 2, 3, 1).reshape(-1, D)          # (B*H*W, D)
        d2 = (flat.pow(2).sum(1, keepdim=True) - 2 * flat @ self.codebook.weight.T
              + self.codebook.weight.pow(2).sum(1))            # squared distances to every code: (B*H*W, K)
        idx = d2.argmin(dim=1)                                 # (B*H*W,) nearest code per position
        z_q = self.codebook(idx).view(B, H, W, D).permute(0, 3, 1, 2)   # (B, D, H, W)
        # codebook loss moves e toward sg[z_e]; commitment loss moves z_e toward sg[e]
        loss = F.mse_loss(z_q, z_e.detach()) + self.beta * F.mse_loss(z_e, z_q.detach())
        z_q = z_e + (z_q - z_e).detach()                       # straight-through: forward = z_q, backward = identity
        return z_q, loss, idx.view(B, H, W)

vq = VectorQuantizer()

Generative adversarial networks

The VAE needs a likelihood, and with a Gaussian or Bernoulli decoder that likelihood treats pixels as independent given zzz, which is a major reason VAE samples are blurry: under squared error the best guess for an uncertain pixel is the average of the possibilities. Goodfellow, Pouget-Abadie, Mirza, Xu, Warde-Farley, Ozair, Courville and Bengio (NeurIPS 2014, "Generative Adversarial Nets") proposed to drop the likelihood and replace it by a learned measure of sample quality: a second network that tries to tell real from fake.

def d_loss(D, real, fake):
    """Discriminator: maximize log D(x) + log(1 - D(G(z))) = minimize BCE with labels 1 (real) and 0 (fake)."""
    real_logits, fake_logits = D(real), D(fake.detach())          # detach: do not update G in the D step

    """Generator: minimize -log D(G(z)), i.e. BCE with the *real* label on the fakes."""
    fake_logits = D(fake)

    """Saturating version, for comparison: minimize log(1 - D(G(z))) = -BCE(fake logits, label 0)."""
    fake_logits = D(fake)

Why GAN training is hard

The game is not an optimization problem with a single loss to decrease. Two networks with opposite goals chase each other, and simultaneous gradient descent on a minimax game can cycle rather than converge. The classic toy example is min⁡xmax⁡yxy\min_x\max_y xyminx​maxy​xy: gradient descent-ascent spirals outward around the saddle point (0,0)(0,0)(0,0). The losses of GGG and DDD therefore do not need to fall, and, unlike a supervised loss, the training curves say little about sample quality: a generator loss that rises can mean the discriminator is improving, or the generator is failing. Typical problems:

  • Vanishing gradients. If DDD becomes too good, its output saturates and gives the generator nothing useful (above), and the deeper reason is below.
  • Non-overlapping supports. Data and samples both lie on low-dimensional manifolds in a huge space, so initially the two distributions have (essentially) disjoint supports. A discriminator can separate them perfectly, and the JS divergence is the constant log⁡2\log2log2 regardless of how far apart they are (see the chart below), so its gradient is uninformative. Arjovsky and Bottou (2017) analyse this carefully, and it motivates the Wasserstein distance.
  • Mode collapse. The generator finds a few outputs that the current discriminator likes and keeps emitting them. The discriminator then learns to reject those, the generator jumps to another mode, and the process cycles without ever covering the data.
  • Oscillation and sensitivity. Small changes in learning rates, normalization, initialization or architecture can decide between success and failure, and results vary widely between random seeds.
  • No convergence criterion. You have to look at samples or use metrics such as FID (below).

Mode collapse

Mode collapse has a clean explanation in terms of the objective. The generator's loss only looks at samples G(z)G(z)G(z) and asks whether they look real, not whether the whole data distribution is covered. Producing a single perfect-looking sample every time gets a good score from a discriminator that evaluates each sample independently (nothing in D(x)D(x)D(x) looks at the batch as a whole). If you view the training as minimizing a divergence, the generator's solution is "reverse-KL-like" (mode seeking, the same effect as in the KL demo above). Mitigations include: minibatch discrimination and minibatch standard deviation features, which let DDD see statistics of a batch and detect lack of diversity (Salimans et al., 2016; Karras et al., 2018); unrolled GANs (Metz et al., 2017), where GGG looks ahead at DDD's response; a better divergence (WGAN); conditional labels; and plain regularization of the discriminator. For distributions on low-dimensional manifolds that never overlap, the difference is dramatic: JS is exactly log⁡2\log2log2 for any separation, while a Wasserstein distance (the cost of moving one pile of probability mass onto another, hence "earth mover's distance") decreases smoothly as the generated samples approach the data.

where Π(p,q)\Pi(p,q)Π(p,q) is the set of joint distributions with marginals ppp and qqq. Computing this directly is intractable, but the Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality turns it into a maximization over 1-Lipschitz functions:

Arjovsky, Chintala and Bottou (ICML 2017, "Wasserstein GAN") parameterize fwf_wfw​ with a network called the critic (it outputs an unbounded real number and not a probability, so it is not a "discriminator"), constrain it to be (approximately) 1-Lipschitz, and train GGG to minimize the critic's estimate of W1W_1W1​. The critic's value is a meaningful quantity: because it estimates a distance, the critic loss correlates with sample quality and can be plotted as a training curve, a rare convenience in the GAN world. Practical consequences of the WGAN framework: train the critic more than the generator (5 critic steps per generator step in the paper), use no sigmoid, and avoid momentum-based optimizers for the critic in the original version (RMSProp).

Enforcing the Lipschitz constraint: clipping, then gradient penalty

The original WGAN enforced the constraint by clipping the critic's weights to [−c,c][-c,c][−c,c] (with c=0.01c=0.01c=0.01), which the authors themselves called a crude hack: it biases the critic towards simple functions, and gradients either vanish or explode depending on ccc. WGAN-GP (Gulrajani, Ahmed, Arjovsky, Dumoulin and Courville, NeurIPS 2017, "Improved Training of Wasserstein GANs") instead penalizes the critic's gradient norm. A differentiable 1-Lipschitz function has gradient norm at most 1 everywhere, and the optimal critic has gradient norm exactly 1 along straight lines between a real sample and its coupled generated sample, so the penalty is evaluated at random points x^=εx+(1−ε)G(z)\hat x=\varepsilon x+(1-\varepsilon)G(z)x^=εx+(1−ε)G(z) with ε∼U[0,1]\varepsilon\sim U[0,1]ε∼U[0,1]:

Lcritic=Ez[fw(G(z))]−Ex[fw(x)]+λ Ex^[(∥∇x^fw(x^)∥2−1)2],λ=10.\mathcal{L}_\text{critic}=\E_{z}[f_w(G(z))]-\E_{x}[f_w(x)]+\lambda\,\E_{\hat x}\Big[\big(\norm{\nabla_{\hat x}f_w(\hat x)}_2-1\big)^2\Big],\qquad\lambda=10 .Lcritic​=Ez​[fw​(G(z))]−Ex​[fw​(x)]+λEx^​[(∥∇x^​fw​(x^)∥2​−1)2],λ=10.

Because the penalty is computed per sample, do not use batch normalization in the critic (it couples samples in a batch); use layer normalization or none. Another way to restrict the Lipschitz constant is spectral normalization (Miyato, Kataoka, Koyama and Yoshida, ICLR 2018), which divides each weight matrix by its largest singular value (estimated by power iteration) and is cheap and effective for ordinary GANs too.

import torch

def gradient_penalty(critic, real, fake, lam=10.0):
    """WGAN-GP penalty on interpolates; real, fake: (B, C, H, W) or (B, d)."""
    B = real.size(0)
    shape = [B] + [1] * (real.dim() - 1)
    eps = torch.rand(shape, device=real.device)                      # one epsilon per sample
    x_hat = (eps * real + (1 - eps) * fake.detach()).requires_grad_(True)
    scores = critic(x_hat)                                           # (B,) or (B, 1)
    grads, = torch.autograd.grad(outputs=scores.sum(), inputs=x_hat,
                                 create_graph=True)                  # create_graph: we backprop through the penalty
    grad_norm = grads.reshape(B, -1).norm(2, dim=1)                  # (B,)
    return lam * ((grad_norm - 1) ** 2).mean()

def critic_loss(critic, real, fake):
    return critic(fake.detach()).mean() - critic(real).mean() + gradient_penalty(critic, real, fake)

def generator_loss(critic, fake):
    return -critic(fake).mean()

DCGAN: architecture rules that made GANs trainable

The original GAN used fully connected networks on MNIST and small images. DCGAN (Radford, Metz and Chintala, ICLR 2016, "Unsupervised Representation Learning with Deep Convolutional Generative Adversarial Networks") listed a handful of architectural guidelines that made convolutional GANs train reliably, and they remain a template:

  • Replace pooling by strided convolutions in the discriminator and transposed convolutions (fractionally strided) in the generator, so the networks learn their own up- and down-sampling.
  • Use batch normalization in both networks (except the generator's output layer and the discriminator's input layer).
  • Remove fully connected hidden layers in deeper architectures.
  • Generator: ReLU activations, tanh at the output (images scaled to [−1,1][-1,1][−1,1]). Discriminator: LeakyReLU (slope 0.2).
  • Optimizer: Adam with learning rate 2×10−42\times10^{-4}2×10−4 and β1=0.5\beta_1=0.5β1​=0.5 (the default β1=0.9\beta_1=0.9β1​=0.9 makes training oscillate).
  • Initialize weights from N(0,0.022)\mathcal{N}(0,0.02^2)N(0,0.022).

The generator for 64×6464\times6464×64 images projects z∈R100z\in\R^{100}z∈R100 to a 4×4×5124\times4\times5124×4×512 tensor and upsamples with four stride-2 transposed convolutions (4→8→16→32→644\to8\to16\to32\to644→8→16→32→64) while halving channels (512→256→128→64→3512\to256\to128\to64\to3512→256→128→64→3). The paper also showed that the latent space is meaningful: vector arithmetic on faces (the average code of "smiling woman" minus "neutral woman" plus "neutral man" decodes to a smiling man), and that interpolating zzz yields smooth transitions rather than fading, evidence that the generator was not memorizing. For MNIST we use a 28×2828\times2828×28 variant in the lab below.

Conditional GANs and image-to-image translation

Conditional GANs

Unconditional GANs give you a random sample. A conditional GAN (Mirza and Osindero, 2014) feeds a label or other conditioning signal yyy to both networks, modelling p(x∣y)p(x\mid y)p(x∣y):

min⁡Gmax⁡D  Ex,y[log⁡D(x∣y)]+Ez,y[log⁡(1−D(G(z∣y)∣y))].\min_G\max_D\;\E_{x,y}[\log D(x\mid y)]+\E_{z,y}[\log(1-D(G(z\mid y)\mid y))].Gmin​Dmax​Ex,y​[logD(x∣y)]+Ez,y​[log(1−D(G(z∣y)∣y))].

For class labels the usual implementation concatenates a one-hot (or learned embedding) of yyy to zzz for GGG and to the input (or a feature map) for DDD. Better-performing variants use projection discriminators (Miyato and Koyama, ICLR 2018), conditional batch normalization and class-conditional spectral normalization, as in BigGAN (Brock, Donahue and Simonyan, ICLR 2019), which scaled class-conditional GANs to ImageNet at 512×512512\times512512×512 and introduced the truncation trick: sample zzz from a truncated normal and trade diversity for fidelity at test time. pix2pix (Isola, Zhu, Zhou and Efros, CVPR 2017) conditions on an image (edges to photo, day to night, labels to street scene): the generator is a U-Net, the discriminator judges local 70×7070\times7070×70 patches ("PatchGAN"), and the loss adds an L1L_1L1​ term between the output and the paired target, so the adversarial loss only has to supply sharp high-frequency detail.

CycleGAN: unpaired translation

Paired data is rare (nobody has a photograph of the same scene as a horse and as a zebra). CycleGAN (Zhu, Park, Isola and Efros, ICCV 2017, "Unpaired Image-to-Image Translation using Cycle-Consistent Adversarial Networks") learns two mappings G:X→YG:X\to YG:X→Y and F:Y→XF:Y\to XF:Y→X between two unpaired image collections, with two discriminators DYD_YDY​ and DXD_XDX​. An adversarial loss on each mapping makes outputs look like the target domain, but that alone is under-constrained (any mapping that produces plausible zebras would do). The key idea is cycle consistency: translating and then translating back should reproduce the input.

L=LGAN(G,DY)+LGAN(F,DX)+λ(Ex∥F(G(x))−x∥1+Ey∥G(F(y))−y∥1),λ=10.\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_\text{GAN}(G,D_Y)+\mathcal{L}_\text{GAN}(F,D_X)+\lambda\Big(\E_x\norm{F(G(x))-x}_1+\E_y\norm{G(F(y))-y}_1\Big),\quad\lambda=10 .L=LGAN​(G,DY​)+LGAN​(F,DX​)+λ(Ex​∥F(G(x))−x∥1​+Ey​∥G(F(y))−y∥1​),λ=10.

CycleGAN works best when the change is textural or local (horse to zebra, summer to winter, photo to painting style) and fails when geometry must change (cat to dog) or when the cycle loss lets the generator hide information in imperceptible high-frequency patterns (steganography) in order to "cheat" the reconstruction. The paper also uses an identity loss for colour preservation in some tasks, and a replay buffer of previously generated images for the discriminators.

StyleGAN

ProGAN (Karras, Aila, Laine and Lehtinen, ICLR 2018, "Progressive Growing of GANs") trained a GAN by starting at 4×44\times44×4 and adding layers that raise the resolution during training, which stabilized the generation of 1024×10241024\times10241024×1024 faces. StyleGAN (Karras, Laine and Aila, CVPR 2019, "A Style-Based Generator Architecture for Generative Adversarial Networks") rethought the generator:

  1. A mapping network (an 8-layer MLP) transforms the noise z∈Zz\in\mathcal{Z}z∈Z into an intermediate latent w∈Ww\in\mathcal{W}w∈W. Z\mathcal{Z}Z must follow a fixed distribution (a Gaussian), but the data distribution of factors such as "age" or "hair length" is not Gaussian; W\mathcal{W}W is free to be warped to make factors more linear and less entangled.
  2. The synthesis network starts from a learned constant 4×4×5124\times4\times5124×4×512 tensor (not from zzz). At each resolution, www is turned by a learned affine layer into a per-channel scale and bias that modulate the feature maps, originally by adaptive instance normalization (AdaIN) and in StyleGAN2 by a weight demodulation that removes the "droplet" artefacts AdaIN created (Karras et al., CVPR 2020).
  3. Noise inputs (per-pixel Gaussian noise, scaled by learned per-channel weights) are added after each convolution to generate stochastic detail such as hair strands and pores that need not be encoded in www.

Because each block receives its own style, style mixing works: use w1w_1w1​ for the coarse blocks (pose, face shape, hair style) and w2w_2w2​ for the fine blocks (colour, micro-texture), and you obtain a face that combines the two. A truncation trick in W\mathcal{W}W (pulling www toward its mean) trades diversity for quality. Later versions fixed artefacts (StyleGAN2), removed aliasing (StyleGAN3, Karras et al., NeurIPS 2021) and made training work on small datasets with adaptive discriminator augmentation (Karras et al., NeurIPS 2020). StyleGAN-type latent spaces remain popular for image editing by moving a code along a learned direction and for GAN inversion (finding the www for a real photo).

Evaluating generative models

Likelihood-based models report bits per dimension or negative log-likelihood on held-out data. For implicit models there is no likelihood, and for GANs even a VAE-style bound does not help. Evaluation therefore uses sample-based metrics, and no single number captures the three things you want: fidelity (samples look real), diversity (they cover the data) and novelty (they are not copies of the training set). Human evaluation remains the gold standard and is expensive.

Inception Score

The Inception Score (Salimans, Goodfellow, Zaremba, Cheung, Radford and Chen, NeurIPS 2016, "Improved Techniques for Training GANs") feeds generated images to an ImageNet-trained Inception network and looks at the class posterior p(y∣x)p(y\mid x)p(y∣x). A good generator should produce images that are recognizable (each p(y∣x)p(y\mid x)p(y∣x) has low entropy) and diverse (the marginal p(y)=Exp(y∣x)p(y)=\E_xp(y\mid x)p(y)=Ex​p(y∣x) has high entropy). Both are captured by

IS=exp⁡(Ex∼pgDKL(p(y∣x) ∥ p(y)))=exp⁡(H(y)−ExH(y∣x)).\mathrm{IS}=\exp\Big(\E_{x\sim p_g}\KL\big(p(y\mid x)\,\|\,p(y)\big)\Big)=\exp\big(H(y)-\E_xH(y\mid x)\big).IS=exp(Ex∼pg​​DKL​(p(y∣x)∥p(y)))=exp(H(y)−Ex​H(y∣x)).

It ranges from 1 (all outputs the same, or unrecognizable) to the number of classes (1000 for ImageNet; confident and perfectly spread). Weaknesses: it never looks at real data, so a generator can obtain a high score while missing the real distribution; it depends on the ImageNet classes (meaningless for faces or medical images); it is blind to intra-class diversity (a generator that produces one perfect image per class gets the maximum); and it is sensitive to the network implementation and image resizing.

Frechet Inception Distance

The FID (Heusel, Ramsauer, Unterthiner, Nessler and Hochreiter, NeurIPS 2017, in the paper that also proposed the two time-scale update rule for GANs) compares real and generated images in the feature space of the Inception network (the 2048-dimensional pooling layer). Fit a Gaussian N(μr,Σr)\mathcal{N}(\mu_r,\Sigma_r)N(μr​,Σr​) to the real features and N(μg,Σg)\mathcal{N}(\mu_g,\Sigma_g)N(μg​,Σg​) to the generated features and compute the Frechet (2-Wasserstein) distance between the Gaussians:

FID=∥μr−μg∥22+Tr⁡(Σr+Σg−2(ΣrΣg)1/2).\mathrm{FID}=\norm{\mu_r-\mu_g}_2^2+\operatorname{Tr}\Big(\Sigma_r+\Sigma_g-2\big(\Sigma_r\Sigma_g\big)^{1/2}\Big).FID=∥μr​−μg​∥22​+Tr(Σr​+Σg​−2(Σr​Σg​)1/2).

Lower is better, with 0 meaning the two Gaussians agree. The first term compares means (is the overall content right?) and the second compares covariances (is the spread and diversity right?), so FID reacts to both quality and mode dropping. It is the standard benchmark (CIFAR-10 FIDs fell from tens to low single digits during the GAN era), but it has limits: it assumes the features are Gaussian, is biased by sample size (always use at least 10,000, ideally 50,000, and report that number), depends on the exact Inception weights and image preprocessing, and can be hacked or misjudge images far from ImageNet. Each number must be paired with the protocol.

pythonfid.py
import numpy as np
from scipy import linalg

def frechet_distance(feats_r, feats_g):
    """feats_*: (N, d) arrays of Inception pool features (d = 2048 for the standard FID)."""
    mu_r, mu_g = feats_r.mean(0), feats_g.mean(0)
    cov_r, cov_g = np.cov(feats_r, rowvar=False), np.cov(feats_g, rowvar=False)
    covmean = linalg.sqrtm(cov_r @ cov_g)                     # matrix square root (can be complex numerically)
    covmean = np.real(covmean)
    return float(((mu_r - mu_g) ** 2).sum() + np.trace(cov_r) + np.trace(cov_g) - 2 * np.trace(covmean))

def inception_score(probs, eps=1e-12):
    """probs: (N, C) softmax outputs p(y|x) of the classifier on generated images."""
    p_y = probs.mean(0, keepdims=True)
    kl = (probs * (np.log(probs + eps) - np.log(p_y + eps))).sum(1)
    return float(np.exp(kl.mean()))

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
a, b = rng.normal(size=(5000, 16)), rng.normal(size=(5000, 16)) + 0.5
print(frechet_distance(a, a[::-1]), frechet_distance(a, b))   # about 0 and about 16 * 0.25 = 4

Precision and recall for distributions

FID collapses fidelity and diversity into one number, and a high FID does not say which one failed. Sajjadi, Bachem, Lucic, Bousquet and Gelly (NeurIPS 2018) and, in the form now commonly used, Kynkaanniemi, Karras, Laine, Lehtinen and Aila (NeurIPS 2019, "Improved Precision and Recall Metric for Assessing Generative Models") split it in two. Embed real and generated samples in a feature space and approximate each distribution's support by the union of balls around its samples, with each ball's radius set by the distance to its kkk-th nearest neighbour (typically k=3k=3k=3):

  • Precision is the fraction of generated samples that fall inside the real manifold: a fidelity measure ("are the samples realistic?").
  • Recall is the fraction of real samples that fall inside the generated manifold: a coverage measure ("does the generator reach everything?").

A mode-collapsed GAN has high precision and low recall; a blurry VAE tends to have lower precision but good recall. This pair makes the quality-diversity trade-off explicit (and shows the effect of the truncation trick, which raises precision and lowers recall). Other metrics worth knowing: KID (kernel inception distance, an unbiased alternative), CLIP-based scores (Week 11) such as CLIPScore for text-conditional generation, LPIPS for perceptual diversity and similarity, and nearest-neighbour checks against the training set for memorization.

python
import numpy as np

def run(mode, eta=0.1, steps=200):
    x, y = 1.0, 1.0
    gx_prev, gy_prev = y, x                     # gradients at the start: dV/dx = y, dV/dy = x
    r = []
    for _ in range(steps):
        gx, gy = y, x
        if mode == "simultaneous":
            x, y = x - eta * gx, y + eta * gy
        elif mode == "alternating":
            x = x - eta * gx
            y = y + eta * x                     # y sees the new x
        else:                                   # optimistic: extrapolate with the last gradient
            x, y = x - eta * (2 * gx - gx_prev), y + eta * (2 * gy - gy_prev)
        gx_prev, gy_prev = gx, gy
        r.append(np.hypot(x, y))
    return r[-1]

The take-away is that the dynamics of a two-player game, not only the loss, determine whether training converges: this is why learning-rate ratios (TTUR), momentum settings, gradient penalties on DDD (e.g. R1, Mescheder et al., ICML 2018) and optimistic methods matter for GANs.

Exercise 10: designing a CycleGAN failure

Describe a concrete pair of image domains for which CycleGAN will not work well, explain which part of the loss is responsible, and suggest a remedy.

Solution

Translating between domains that differ in geometry or shape, such as cats to dogs, or a photograph of a person to a different pose. The cycle-consistency loss rewards mappings that are nearly invertible pixel-wise, which encourages local texture changes that preserve structure, and the adversarial loss on its own is satisfied by any plausible image of the target domain. The generator is therefore pushed to change colours and textures and leave the shape. Another failure is steganography: the generator hides the information required for the reverse mapping in low-amplitude patterns. Remedies include paired data if any exists (pix2pix), shape-aware architectures with larger receptive fields, a perceptual or feature-space cycle loss, adding noise to the generator's outputs before the reverse mapping, or moving to diffusion-based translation methods.

Key takeaways

Key takeaways
  • Generative models differ in what they let you compute: autoregressive models and flows give exact likelihoods, VAEs give a bound, GANs give only samples, and the choice trades off sample quality, coverage and speed.
  • Autoencoders learn a code by reconstruction through a bottleneck (or noise, or sparsity); a linear autoencoder spans the PCA subspace. A plain autoencoder has no prior over codes, so it is not a generative model.
  • The ELBO is Eq[log⁡p(x∣z)]−DKL(q(z∣x)∥p(z))\E_q[\log p(x\mid z)]-\KL(q(z\mid x)\|p(z))Eq​[logp(x∣z)]−DKL​(q(z∣x)∥p(z)), a lower bound whose gap is DKL(q(z∣x)∥p(z∣x))\KL(q(z\mid x)\|p(z\mid x))DKL​(q(z∣x)∥p(z∣x)). The KL to a standard normal is 12∑(μ2+σ2−1−log⁡σ2)\tfrac12\sum(\mu^2+\sigma^2-1-\log\sigma^2)21​∑(μ2+σ2−1−logσ2).
  • The reparameterization trick, z=μ+σϵz=\mu+\sigma\epsilonz=μ+σϵ, moves the randomness out of the computation graph and makes the expectation differentiable with a low-variance gradient.
  • β\betaβ weights rate against distortion. Large β\betaβ or a strong decoder leads to posterior collapse; remedies are KL warm-up, free bits and weaker decoders. VQ-VAE uses discrete codes, a straight-through estimator and a separate prior, and avoids the KL altogether.
  • For a fixed generator the optimal discriminator is pdata/(pdata+pg)p_\text{data}/(p_\text{data}+p_g)pdata​/(pdata​+pg​), and the minimax value is −log⁡4+2 JS(pdata∥pg)-\log4+2\,\mathrm{JS}(p_\text{data}\|p_g)−log4+2JS(pdata​∥pg​). The non-saturating generator loss avoids vanishing gradients early on.
  • GAN training is a game and may cycle; mode collapse, vanishing gradients and sensitivity to hyperparameters are typical. WGAN replaces JS with the Wasserstein distance, enforced via 1-Lipschitz critics (gradient penalty or spectral normalization).
  • DCGAN's recipe (strided convolutions, batch norm, LeakyReLU, Adam with β1=0.5\beta_1=0.5β1​=0.5) is the template for stable convolutional GANs; conditional GANs, pix2pix and CycleGAN add control, and StyleGAN adds a mapping network and per-layer styles.
  • Evaluate with FID (fidelity and diversity, protocol-dependent), Inception Score (classifier-dependent, blind to real data), and precision and recall (which separate fidelity from coverage), and always look at samples.
